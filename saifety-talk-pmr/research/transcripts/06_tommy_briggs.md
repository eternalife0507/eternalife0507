## Section 1 — Warm-up

**Interviewer (W1):** Walk me through yesterday, from the moment you got to the gate until you left. What took most of your attention?

**Tommy:** Yesterday's Saturday, so it's overtime. We run four tens Monday to Thursday and then Saturdays when the sequence is behind, which is basically every Saturday since June. I'm at the gate about quarter to six. Badge in, and that's a whole thing on this job, the badges, I'll get to it. Walk to our gang box, coffee, and I've got maybe eighteen, nineteen guys on Saturdays across two raising gangs, a bolt-up crew and a decking crew. Six-thirty we do the pre-task, the JHA. Paper. Foremen go over it with their gangs, I walk between them.

What took my attention? The picks. Always the picks. We're erecting steel on one of the support buildings, the utility building, and the GC's got a mechanical contractor setting equipment on the other side, and they're both living off crane time. So half my day is standing there watching whose load is in the air over whose guys. Yesterday wasn't bad. Yesterday I only had to go find the lift coordinator once.

**Interviewer (W1 probe):** What happened that one time?

**Tommy:** Their crawler was boomed out setting a duct section and the swing was coming across the east end where my decking crew was laying sheets. Nobody told us that pick was happening. My foreman on the deck pulled his guys back to the column line and radioed me, and I walked over to the lift coordinator's pickup and said, you know, "what the hell." He said it was on the lift plan. Maybe it was. It wasn't on the one I got at six-thirty. We lost maybe forty minutes. Nobody got hurt. That's a good day, if you want to know.

**Interviewer (W2):** Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day: other trades, other contractors, other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

**Tommy:** The GC's area superintendent for our building. The GC safety rep assigned to our area. The lift coordinator. Our own company's super and PM, who are in a trailer and I see them maybe twice a day. The crane operators, obviously, they're operating engineers, different local, good guys mostly. Then whoever's around us. Right now that's the mechanical guys, some electricians coming in behind us on the lower levels, the fireproofing sub, and the concrete guys who pour on our deck.

How does it happen? Face to face, mostly. Radio for the crane stuff. There's a two o'clock coordination meeting in the GC trailer every day that I'm supposed to go to and I go to about half of them, because at two o'clock I've usually got steel in the air. Our company super goes. Stuff gets decided at that meeting that nobody tells the guys on the iron. That's the honest answer.

**Interviewer (W2 probe):** Can you give me an example of something decided there that didn't reach the crew?

**Tommy:** Couple weeks ago they moved the laydown for the mechanical contractor's equipment right next to our shakeout area. Decided at two o'clock on a Tuesday. Wednesday morning there's a forklift weaving through my guys while they're shaking out beams. My foreman didn't know, I didn't know. Our super knew. He said he "figured it was obvious." Nothing's obvious on a job this size.

**Interviewer (W3):** When something on site looks "off" to you or your crew, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

**Tommy:** Depends what it is. If it's ours, it's ours, we fix it, that's the trade. A guy sees a column with a bad anchor bolt or a beam that came in with the wrong holes, we deal with that inside the company. If it's somebody else's and it's going to hurt one of my guys, I'm going to stop the work and I'm going to go get the GC safety rep in person. I don't text him. I go get him and I bring him to it and I make him look at it.

Most recent. Thursday. The electricians had pulled a section of perimeter cable on the second level to get a conduit run through, and they didn't put it back. My connector saw it when he came down the column. He told his foreman, his foreman told me, I went and got the safety rep, the rep got the electrical foreman, and the cable was back up in about an hour. And I wrote it in my book.

**Interviewer (W3 probe):** What's in your book?

**Tommy:** Date, time, what it was, where, who I told, what they said. That's it. It's a little waterproof notebook, I've got a stack of them at home going back years. That book's not for the GC. That book's for when something goes sideways and somebody says "nobody told us." Somebody told you. It's right here.

**Interviewer (W4):** What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

**Tommy:** Paper JHA, every morning, signed. Radio. My phone for calls and texts. The notebook. There's the union app, for dues and dispatch, and that's the union's app on my phone, that's fine. The erection drawings and the sequence, our company gives me those on paper and on a tablet in the gang box that I use for drawings and nothing else.

Likes and dislikes. The radio, I like that it's instant. I don't like that three trades share our channel and half of what's on there is noise. Paper JHA, honestly, the paper JHA is only as good as the foreman doing it. Some guys, it's a real conversation. Other guys, it's sign here, sign here, go. The notebook I like because it's mine.

What I don't use, and I'll tell you this now since you asked: I don't put company apps on my phone. Not the GC's, not ours. My guys don't either, not if they ask me. **Interviewer (W4 probe):** Why the line at personal phones?

**Tommy:** Because it's my phone. It's got my family on it, my location, everything. I don't know what that app's pulling off it and neither do you. They said it was for mustering, for emergencies. Fine, we said, then put it in writing that that's all it's for. It took four months to get that language and I'm still not sure they follow it.

## Section 2 — Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your crew or site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Tommy:** Formally write it up. *(laughs)* Most things don't get formally written up, and that's not always bad. The perimeter cable Thursday, that never got "written up" as far as I know. It got fixed. That's what I care about.

But you mean somebody who saw something and kept his mouth shut. Yeah. About three weeks ago. I had a first-year apprentice on the decking crew. He's working the east bay, and there's a hole in the deck that the mechanical guys cut for a pipe riser. The cover's on it but it's a piece of plywood, not marked, not secured. Kid sees it at like seven in the morning. Doesn't say a word. I find out at lunch because one of the journeymen stepped on the edge of it and it shifted. He didn't go through, thank God. And I asked the kid, did you see that this morning? And he goes, "yeah, I figured somebody knew."

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why do you think he didn't say anything?

**Tommy:** Couple reasons. He's a first-year. He's been on the iron for maybe five months. The first-years, they don't want to be the guy. They're scared of the journeymen, they're scared of the foreman, they're scared of me a little, probably. And it wasn't our hole, so he figured it's not his business. That's the other thing, a lot of these kids think if another company made it, it's another company's problem.

And the travelers are the same, maybe worse. We've got a lot of travelers on this job. Guys out of other locals, from all over, because the job's too big for 172 to man by itself. A traveler knows when the job slows down, he's the first one on the list. So he's not going to be the guy stopping picks. He'll mention it to his foreman quietly, maybe. He's not going over anybody.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** What would have happened to him if he had reported it formally?

**Tommy:** To the kid? Probably nothing bad from us. From me, he'd have got a pat on the back. But he doesn't know that, and that's on us, honestly, that's on me. From the GC side, depending on who took it, maybe somebody would have come over and asked him fifteen questions about where he was standing and was he tied off. That happens. You report a hole and the first question back is about your harness. That's the part that makes guys quiet.

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Tommy:** Bad one first, because it's the one I remember. This isn't this job. This was a power plant, few years back. We had a connector, good hand, and he kept flagging that the GC wasn't getting the horizontal lifelines up ahead of us. Kept flagging it. Told the foreman, told safety. So one day the GC's safety guy is doing a walk, and he takes a picture of this connector moving between two beams, and in that couple of seconds he's not tied off because there's nothing to tie off to. The lifeline that he'd been asking for wasn't there. They terminated him. For a "safety violation."

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** What happened next?

**Tommy:** I filed a grievance. Our BA took it up. We had his emails, well, not emails, we had my notebook, dates he raised it, who he told, and we had the GC's own lift plan that said the lifelines were supposed to be installed. He got reinstated with back pay. We won that. But he was out of work for six weeks, and every guy on that job saw what happened to the guy who spoke up. You win the grievance and you still lose, a little, because everybody learned the lesson they weren't supposed to learn.

**Interviewer (C2 probe, F-Tommy):** How does the union get involved, generally, when something like that happens?

**Tommy:** Member comes to me, or I see it myself. If it's a safety thing that I can fix right there, I fix it right there. I'll stop the work. If I think there's retaliation behind it, meaning somebody's getting moved or written up or laid off because he raised something, that goes to the business agent that day. Not the next day. The BA knows the GC's labor relations people. We've got a labor-management safety committee on this site too, meets monthly, and I sit on it. That's where I bring the pattern stuff. Individual cases go to the BA. Patterns go to the committee.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Has it ever affected someone's assignments or layoffs on this site?

**Tommy:** On this site, I can't prove it. I'll say that carefully. I've got one guy, a traveler, who raised a rigging issue on the mechanical contractor's picks back in the spring. Within two weeks he's moved from the raising gang to the shakeout yard. Our own company moved him, not the GC. They said it was manpower. Maybe it was. It went in my book.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard you or your crew reported in the last month. What did you see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Tommy:** The plywood hole. After the journeyman stepped on it I went and got the safety rep. He had the mechanical foreman out there in maybe twenty minutes, and the cover got replaced, a proper one, marked and cleated down, that afternoon. That one worked, because I stood there.

Another one, in the same month. I asked the GC to look at the access to the second level on the north side. The stair tower wasn't done, and guys were using a ladder that was tied off at the top with, I'm not kidding, tie wire. I told the safety rep in person. He said he'd get it handled. That was, let me think, about a month ago. I didn't hear anything. Nine days later I'm walking by and it's the same ladder with the same tie wire. So I told him again, and that time I stood there until somebody came. It got fixed that day.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** How did that affect whether you'd report again?

**Tommy:** Me? It doesn't. I'm going to report. That's my job, I'm the steward, and frankly I'm a loud son of a gun. It's the other guys. If one of my journeymen reports the ladder and nothing happens for nine days, next time he doesn't bother. He figures, why, it's Tommy's thing. Everything ends up coming through me, and that's not a system, that's one guy. What happens when I'm not there?

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What's the longest you've waited?

**Tommy:** Never heard back at all, lots of times. You find out it got fixed because you walk past it and it's fixed. Nobody ever comes back and says "hey, that thing you raised, we took care of it." I can count on one hand the times a GC safety guy closed the loop with me without me asking. And one time, this job, the safety rep had it on his iPad as "closed." I said, closed how? It's still right there. He said the other contractor marked it closed. So somebody clicked a button.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report: cards, quotas, rewards, apps? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Tommy:** Oh, all of it. Where do you want to start.

Cards. On this job, every foreman is supposed to turn in so many observation cards a week. Five, I think it is now. What happens is Friday afternoon a foreman sits in the truck and fills out five cards. "Observed worker wearing safety glasses. Positive." That's a card. It counts. And then the ones that aren't positive, those are the ones that worry me, because those cards have a guy's behavior on them. "Observed worker not wearing gloves." That's the behavior-based stuff, and my problem with behavior-based safety is it's always about the guy. It's never about the condition. A guy gets written up for his glove while there's a crane swinging a load over his head because somebody else needed the pick. Which one of those is going to kill him? The glove isn't going to kill him.

Rewards. The site has a thing where if the building goes a certain number of hours with no recordables, there's a barbecue, there's gift cards. You know what that does. I've seen a guy, not this job, working with a busted finger for three days because he didn't want to be the guy who cost everybody the barbecue. That's not safety, that's hiding injuries with a cookout on top.

And the app. Last year the GC rolled out a sticker on the hard hats with a code, and you were supposed to scan it and put in observations. I told my members not to download it on their own phones. Some of the other crafts did. It was big for maybe a month. By about two months in, nobody was using it except the GC's own people.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why do you think it faded?

**Tommy:** Nobody trusted where it went. That's number one. Number two, nobody heard back. You put something in, it goes in a hole. And number three, it was a quota thing again, the foremen were getting asked why their guys weren't putting in enough. As soon as it's a number somebody's graded on, it's garbage. The guys know that.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** What did people say about it in the break area?

**Tommy:** *(laughs)* I can't repeat most of it. The clean version: "the snitch sticker." That's what the guys called it. You'd see guys with a piece of electrical tape over it.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment you notice a hazard mid-task. Where are you, what's in your hands, what's around you, and how much time do you realistically have to tell someone?

**Tommy:** Depends who. I'll tell you about a connector, because that's what I did for fifteen years. You're up on the steel. You've got a harness on, two lanyards, maybe a beamer. You've got a spud wrench and a bolt bag. Your hands are on the iron or on the load. There's a load coming at you on the hook. The noise is impact guns and the crane and the wind, and up top in Ohio in November the wind is no joke. You are not taking your phone out. You are not taking your gloves off. If you see something, you yell to your partner and you yell down, or you get your foreman on the radio when you're in a safe spot.

A decking crew, it's a bit different, they're on a working surface, but it's the same thing, gloves, noise, and you're moving sheets.

Time to tell someone? If it's life safety, you've got no time, you just stop and you yell. If it's something you notice, like a missing toe board or a cable that's slack, you tell your foreman at the next break. Coffee, lunch. And by then half the time you forgot about it, or somebody else fixed it, or it's somebody else's problem.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What about phone rules on the site?

**Tommy:** You're not supposed to be on your phone on the iron. Period. Our company rule and the GC's rule. On the ground, in the break area, fine. I use mine all day for calls, but I'm the GF, I'm not connecting anymore.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What's the longest you'd spend before you'd deal with it later?

**Tommy:** I'm going to give you an answer you don't want, probably. It's not about seconds. You people, whoever you are, always want to know how many seconds. Seconds don't matter to me. A guy will take five minutes to report something if he knows nothing bad's going to happen to him. He won't take five seconds if he thinks it's going to come back on him. The time isn't the problem. The consequence is the problem.

---

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

**Tommy:** Okay. My guy sees it. He tells his foreman. Foreman tells me, or if it's small the foreman handles it himself. I go get the GC safety rep, in person. Safety rep either fixes it himself if it's small, or he calls the area super, or he goes and finds the other contractor's foreman. Other contractor's foreman sends somebody to fix it. Or doesn't.

Where it slows down: two places. One is the GC. If the safety rep has to go through the area super, and the area super looks at it and thinks about his schedule first, it sits. The other is the other contractor. Their foreman's got his own production, and my hazard isn't his priority. He'll say "we'll get it after lunch," and after lunch becomes tomorrow.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Who decides who owns it?

**Tommy:** The GC safety rep, in theory. In practice it's whoever gets blamed first. And that goes wrong. We had a deck opening a couple months back, the GC put it on us, "ironworkers left an opening." It wasn't ours. It was a penetration another trade cut after we'd laid and welded that deck. But it sat on our company's list for a week, and our PM got a nasty email, before anybody went and looked at who actually cut it. Meanwhile the thing's still open.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Any duplicates, or reports missing information like location?

**Tommy:** Oh, sure. Three guys from three crafts will tell three different people about the same thing, and it gets fixed once, and maybe it gets closed three times or maybe it never gets closed, depending on whose list it's on. And location, yeah. "There's a hole on level two." Level two of what? This campus has a dozen buildings going up. Level two where? Somebody's walking around for twenty minutes looking for it.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or an instruction was passed along *automatically*, or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Tommy:** Automatically. The badges. I said I'd get to it. So we got language that the location badges are for mustering and emergencies. Then back in the summer, one of my members, a journeyman, gets called into our company office because the badge system "flagged" him in a zone he wasn't assigned to. That report went from the GC's system straight to our company. Nobody talked to him first. Turns out he'd walked through that area to get to the porta-johns because the path on our side was blocked by a laydown. So there's a guy getting called into the office by a computer.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** How did people react?

**Tommy:** I went through the roof. That went to the BA the same day and to the labor-management committee that month. The GC said it was a "configuration error." They fixed the configuration. But every guy on that job now knows the badge is watching him, no matter what the paper says. So you asked how people react. They stop believing anything that says "this is only for safety."

The other kind, somebody who doesn't know the area. There's a young safety coordinator on the GC side, nice kid, never worked with tools. He forwards things around like a mailman. He sent an observation about "ironworkers" to our company that was actually the rebar guys. Different contractor, they're reinforcing ironworkers, not us. Our PM called me about it like I'd done something.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore an automatically routed item?

**Tommy:** I'd ignore anything automatic by default. Trust? It'd have to be that a person who knows the job looked at it before it went anywhere. And it'd have to be about the condition, not about a person. The moment a thing routes a man's name to his boss, I don't care how smart it is, I'm against it.

**Interviewer (C8 probe, F-Tommy):** Who should see the reporter's identity?

**Tommy:** Nobody in management, unless the guy says so. The guy who reports decides. If he wants his name on it so he gets credit, fine, that's his choice. If he doesn't, it's anonymous, really anonymous, not "anonymous but the safety department can see it." The steward sees it if the member wants the steward to see it. And the union, I'll say this, the union should see the same data the GC sees. Not names. The counts, what was reported, what got fixed, how long it took. If the GC's allowed to look at the pattern, so are we.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Tommy:** Crane picks. That's the story on this whole job. The big one: about six weeks ago. We're erecting on the utility building. The mechanical contractor has a crawler on the west side setting air handler sections, and their swing radius crosses over our deck on the west bays. **Interviewer (C9 probe):** How was it discovered?

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** How often does that kind of overlap happen in a week?

**Tommy:** Some kind of overlap, a couple times a week. Crane time, laydown space, access. Something that I'd call serious, where somebody could get killed, maybe once every two weeks on this job. And who owns the conflict? It's supposed to be the GC. The lift coordinator. In practice it's whoever's willing to stand there and stop the job. Which is me, most of the time.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Tommy:** It's the same one. The crane stop-work six weeks ago. That was the second one I've led on this job, both over crane lifts.

**Tommy:** Known? It was known for three days. By my decking crew, by me, by the GC safety rep, I told him twice. I'd bet the crane operator knew, those guys aren't stupid. I don't know what a day costs them in dollars. That's not my number. My number is zero guys hurt.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, for example your crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Tommy:** It gets closed when somebody who cares walks over and sees it closed. That's the honest answer. The perimeter cable Thursday: electricians took it down, my guy saw it, the GC got the electrical foreman, it went back up. Who proved it's closed? My foreman looked at it and told me. I went up and looked at it myself at the end of the day. That's closed.

Sometimes it's worse. Sometimes the fastest way to close it is my guys fix it themselves, like re-hang a cable another trade pulled, and then there's a fight about whose time that was, and whether we get back-charged or they do. And meanwhile, if it's between employers and nobody walks it, it just sits.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help, or don't?

**Tommy:** The GC has it in Procore or whatever they use. I don't have access to that. I've asked. The union doesn't have access to it. So they can mark something "closed" and I can't see it and I can't check it. My notebook helps me, because I know what I told who and when. But the notebook doesn't close anything. Honestly, the tool that works is a guy with a radio and the authority to stop the work. Everything else is paperwork about that.

## Section 3 — Concept Presentation & Perceived Value

The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Tommy:** *(long pause)* So it's an app.

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'd like to hear your reaction to the description as I read it.

**Tommy:** Yeah. So it's an app. And the "system suggests," that's a computer doing the sorting. Okay. Ask your questions.

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Tommy:** For me? Probably not much. I don't have a problem getting heard. I walk over and I stand there until it's fixed. I'm not the one who needs this.

What it might change: the kid with the plywood hole. The traveler who's not going to stop picks. If he could say something without it being his name on it, and then actually hear back that it got fixed, that's something. What it wouldn't change: the crane problem. The crane problem is not a reporting problem. Everybody knew about the picks over my deck. It got reported, by me, twice, in person. The problem was the GC didn't want to lose the pick. No app fixes that. **Interviewer (P1 probe):** Anything else it wouldn't change?

**Tommy:** It won't change the card quotas, if that's what you're worried about. It'll be one more thing unless something else goes away. And it won't change who decides whose fault it is, it just moves that to the "suggests a responsible owner" part. Which, I've got thoughts on.

**Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you or your crew refuse to use it?

**Tommy:** Where do I start. Okay.

One. Who owns what goes in there. Who can see it, who can pull it, how long they keep it. If a guy puts in a voice report, is that his voice, recorded, sitting on a server somewhere that the company's lawyer can pull in a year?
