## Section 1 — Warm-up

**Interviewer (Intro):** Thanks for making the time, Marcus. This is a research conversation about how safety and field coordination work on large projects today. There are no right or wrong answers, and I'm not here to sell you anything. I'll take notes, and nothing you say will be attributed to you or your company. Is that okay?

**Marcus:** That's fine. I've got a hard stop at the top of the hour, so we'll keep it moving.

**Interviewer (W1):** Walk me through yesterday from the moment you got to the gate until you left. What took most of your attention?

**Marcus:** Yesterday was Saturday, so yesterday was my daughter's soccer game and two phone calls. Let me give you Friday, that's more representative.

Friday I was at our Virginia campus. That's the biggest of the four data center jobs I've got right now, about 2,800 craft on site at the moment, three buildings, one of them in commissioning. I badged in around 6:15. First thing was the project's morning safety huddle with the site safety manager and his coordinators, fifteen minutes. Then about two hours in the trailer on a call with our analyst going over the draft of the September roll-up, because the owner's monthly review is Tuesday. Afternoon was a SIF precursor review on a near-miss from Wednesday, a scissor lift that got into a trench edge by the generator yard. No injury. Then I drove back and did email until about seven.

What took most of my attention, honestly, was the roll-up. The walk is the part of the job I like. The roll-up is the part that eats me.

**Interviewer (W1 probe):** What about the roll-up took the attention?

**Marcus:** The numbers didn't tie. The Virginia site's observation count in Procore said one thing, the count the GC coordinators reported in their weekly said another, and the enterprise system had a third number because only a subset gets pushed there. My analyst spent most of Thursday reconciling it and we still had a 14 percent gap we couldn't explain. So I'm about to stand in front of an owner on Tuesday with a leading-indicator slide I don't fully trust. That's not a great feeling for a guy whose name is on it.

**Interviewer (W2):** Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day: other trades, other contractors or other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

**Marcus:** My day is mostly people, not trades. Five site safety managers, one per project, they're my direct line to the field. The project directors on each job. My VP of Ops, who I report to. Corporate EHS, which owns the standards and the enterprise system. The owners' HSE leads, and on hyperscale jobs the owner is very present, they have their own people walking. Then subcontractor safety leadership on the big subs, the electrical and mechanical contractors especially, maybe six or seven companies that matter across the portfolio. And our IT people more than I'd like, because every site wants some new tool.

How it happens: Teams calls, mostly. A weekly one-on-one with each site safety manager. The monthly regional HSE council, which I chair. Quarterly SIF reviews. And a lot of texts at odd hours when something happens.

**Interviewer (W2 probe):** Can you give me an example of a coordination from last week that didn't go the way it should have?

**Marcus:** Sure. The owner's HSE lead on the Georgia campus emailed me directly, not the site, asking why a dropped-object near-miss they saw on their own walk wasn't in our monthly report. It turned out it was in Procore as an observation, logged by a sub, but it had been categorized as "housekeeping" so it never got flagged as an SIF precursor and never made the enterprise system. So the owner found out about our near-miss before I did. That's the thing I hate most in this job, somebody above me knowing something about my projects before I do.

**Interviewer (W3):** When something on site looks "off" to you or your crew, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

**Marcus:** I don't have a crew, but when I see something on a walk, it goes like this. Friday, on the commissioning walk, I saw a temporary power cord run through a doorway that had a fire door propped on it. Pinched cord, propped fire door, in a building where they're about to do integrated systems testing. Small thing, but it's the kind of small thing.

I pointed it out to the site safety manager, he pointed it out to the electrical sub's foreman who was right there, the foreman had a guy fix it in about five minutes. The coordinator took a photo on his phone. Whether that gets logged anywhere depends on whether the coordinator remembers to put it in Procore at the end of the day. Probably he did because I was standing there. If I hadn't been there, I'd say 50/50.

**Interviewer (W3 probe):** Why 50/50?

**Marcus:** Because it got fixed. Once it's fixed, nobody sees the point of writing it up. From the field's point of view, the paperwork is for things that are still broken. From my point of view, that cord is data. If I see the same thing on four campuses, that's a pattern, and the pattern is what I'd want in front of the regional council. But I only see patterns in what gets written down.

**Interviewer (W4):** What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

**Marcus:** For me: the enterprise EHS system, which is one of the big Intelex-type platforms. That's the system of record for incidents, recordables, first aids, SIF events, investigations, corrective actions. I like that it's auditable and corporate trusts it. I dislike that nobody in the field will touch it. It's built for people like me and my analyst, not for a foreman.

Procore on each project, which is where the GC coordinators log observations and inspections. It's good at project stuff, and the project teams live in it. But every project configures it a little differently, the categories drift, and when the project demobilizes, that data goes into an archive and I basically never see it again.

A couple of our sites also use SafetyCulture for inspection checklists. Fine tool. It's another island.

PowerBI, where the monthly roll-up lives. I like the output. I don't trust the plumbing.

Then Teams, Outlook, and my iPad for walks. I take notes and photos on the iPad and then retype half of it. Permits are a mix: two of the campuses have an electronic permit-to-work system the owner mandated, the others are paper permits on a board in the trailer.

**Interviewer (W4 probe):** You said you don't trust the plumbing. What specifically?

**Marcus:** Three things. Categories don't match across sites, so "struck-by" on one job is "dropped object" on another and "housekeeping" on a third. Location is free text, and "L2 near column" on a million-square-foot building is useless. And the counts depend on who remembered to enter what. My analyst, she's good, spends about a week every month stitching Procore exports, the enterprise system and subs' spreadsheets together. That's a quarter of a skilled person's time spent making numbers agree, not making anything safer.

**Interviewer (W5):** How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

**Marcus:** Tiered. Anything serious, a recordable, a SIF event, a SIF-potential near-miss, a property damage event over a threshold, I get a phone call from the site safety manager within the hour, and I expect it in the enterprise system within 24 hours with the precursor review started. That part works pretty well. Everyone knows the rule.

Below that, I find out through the weekly one-on-ones, the monthly roll-up and walks. Which means the middle band, things that aren't incidents but are more than housekeeping, I see late or not at all. That middle band is where your SIF precursors hide. The Georgia example I gave you is exactly that.

**Interviewer (W5 probe):** How late is late, typically?

**Marcus:** For the middle band? If it's in Procore and categorized right, I'd see it in the monthly, so anywhere from a few days to four weeks. If it's categorized wrong, maybe never, unless an owner tells me. If it was only said out loud on site, never.

## Section 2 — Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your crew or site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Marcus:** The one that I'll never stop talking about is our SIF event last year. Dropped object, Ohio campus. A section of strut, about four feet, fell from the cable tray level, roughly 38 feet, and landed maybe six feet from a laborer. No injury. That's pure luck, not a system.

In the investigation we found the toe board along that section of catwalk had been pulled by the mechanical sub to get a pipe run through, four days earlier. Two electricians working that level had noticed it. One of them told his foreman. The foreman told the mechanical foreman in passing. Nobody wrote it down. Nothing went into Procore. The mechanical foreman said he thought it was already being handled. Four days.

That triggered a regional stand-down across all my projects. And the thing that got me in the investigation is that everyone did the "normal" thing. The electrician told his boss. The boss told the other boss. That is how a construction site works. It just doesn't create a record or an owner.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why didn't the electricians or their foreman write it up?

**Marcus:** A few reasons, from the interviews. One, it wasn't their hazard. It was the mechanical sub's toe board, and there's a real reluctance to put another contractor's name on a report. Two, the foreman said he figured a face-to-face was faster than "the card." Which, frankly, it is. Three, and this one came out later, the electrical sub's safety rep told us their company had gotten back-charged on a previous job after one of their observations led to a finger-pointing fight between two subs. So there's a commercial hesitation. You write up another sub, and suddenly it's a contract issue, not a safety issue.

**Interviewer (C1 probe 2):** What would have happened to the foreman if he had written it up?

**Marcus:** Officially? Nothing, or he'd get a thank-you. We run a just culture model and I believe in it. Unofficially, I think he'd have gotten a call from the mechanical superintendent asking why he didn't just come to him, and maybe a little frost in the next coordination meeting. Not retaliation in the legal sense. Social cost. It's real, I won't pretend it isn't, but I'd rank it behind "it wasn't my hazard" and "I told somebody, so I'm done."

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Marcus:** Good example first, because I want to be fair. On the Texas campus, an apprentice on a drywall crew stopped a crane pick because the tag line guy was standing under the load path. His foreman backed him, the GC safety manager put him up at the all-hands, the crane contractor's super actually shook his hand. That's how it's supposed to go.

Bad example. About eighteen months ago, on a job I'm not going to name, a merit-shop sub's foreman raised a series of issues about another sub's scaffolding. Legitimate issues. Within a few weeks, his own company moved him to a different site. The company said it was manpower. Maybe it was. We looked into it and couldn't prove otherwise. But everybody on that site drew their own conclusion, and the observation count from that sub dropped by about half the following month. I saw that in the roll-up before I knew why.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Who hears about it when someone raises something like that?

**Marcus:** Their foreman, their company's safety rep, the GC coordinator, and depending on the tool, the other contractor. Everybody, basically. There's no real anonymity in construction. If it's a crew of eight and the report is about the thing twenty feet from that crew, people know.

And I'll be honest about something that's on me. My own metrics can make it worse. I report closure rate and observations by contractor to owners. So a sub whose name is on a lot of observations, even as the reporter, starts to look noisy. Some of the sub PMs have told me point blank they coach their foremen to handle things verbally so they don't "light up the dashboard." I haven't solved that. The quality-controlled volume is the leading indicator. Raw volume isn't. But subs don't read my footnotes.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard someone on your projects reported in the last month. What did the reporter see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Marcus:** I'll give you the data version and then an example. Across the portfolio, for observations that are in Procore and have a closure date, median time-to-close is around six days. For items where the reporter's company isn't the company that has to fix it, the cross-contractor ones, it's closer to eleven. And about a fifth of those cross-contractor items have no closure photo, just a status flipped to "closed." Who closed it, and how do we know it's fixed? I can't tell you from the record.

Example. Early this month on the Georgia campus an electrician reported a missing handrail on a stair tower to the roof. The report went to the GC coordinator the same day. The GC assigned it to the scaffold contractor. The scaffold contractor fixed it in two days. The electrician found out it was fixed when he walked the stair a week later. Nobody told him. That's a good outcome with a bad feedback loop.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** How does that affect whether people report again?

**Marcus:** I can't prove it from data, and I'm a data guy, so I want to be careful. What I hear from my site safety managers is that the crews that report the most are the ones who've seen the most things fixed with their name attached to the fix. The ones who report into a void stop. That's anecdote, but it's consistent anecdote across five sites.

**Interviewer (C3 probe 2):** What's the longest you know of that someone waited?

**Marcus:** A housekeeping-coded item on the fab package, oil leak under a temporary generator, open for 41 days. It got closed when the generator got demobilized. That's not closure. That's the problem leaving.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report: cards, quotas, rewards, apps? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Marcus:** Everything. I've been doing this 27 years.

Observation card quotas. Two cards per worker per week on one program years ago. You got a mountain of cards and by week four they were pencil-whipped in the parking lot on Friday. "Worker wearing PPE. Good job." Useless.

Rewards. Gift card raffles for reporters, a truck giveaway on a power plant job once. Spike, then decay. And people start reporting for the raffle, so quality drops.

Apps. Yeah, we've done apps. In 2024 we piloted a near-miss reporting app on one campus. Launch week was huge, because the vendor had people on site handing out stickers. By week ten, weekly active reporters were about 15 percent of launch week. By week sixteen we turned it off. And we had a computer-vision camera pilot, PPE detection, on another campus. That generated thousands of alerts a week, nobody had the staff to triage them, the union on that site thought it was surveillance, and it died quietly after the pilot term.

I've sat through, I don't know, twenty of these pitches in the last three years. Cameras, wearables, chatbots. My first question to every one of them is "what happens in week 12?" Most of them don't have an answer, or they show me week 2.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why do you think those faded?

**Marcus:** Two reasons, mostly. The reporting side was easy and the closing side wasn't, so the reports piled up somewhere and the workers noticed nothing changed. And each one was a separate island. The data never made it into the enterprise system, so to corporate it didn't exist, and when the pilot champion on site rotated off, the tool had no owner. The one thing that never failed was the novelty wearing off. That's guaranteed.

**Interviewer (C4 probe 2):** What did people say about it in the trailer or break room?

**Marcus:** On the app, the line I heard from a GF was, "I send it, it goes to the cloud, the cloud doesn't fix handrails." That stuck with me.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment someone notices a hazard mid-task: where are they, what's in their hands, what's around them, and how much time do they realistically have to tell someone?

**Marcus:** I'm not on the tools, so take this as what I see on walks. On a data center, a lot of the work is at height, on lifts or on the tray level. They've got gloves on, often cut-level gloves, and a tool in hand. It's loud in the generator yard and the electrical rooms during testing. And a detail people outside the industry miss: on hyperscale jobs, a lot of owners restrict phone cameras. In the buildings that are live or near live, some owners ban photos entirely, or only allow photos on a GC-issued device. So "just take a picture" is not always allowed.

Realistic time? For a crew member, I'd say they'll give it about as long as it takes to yell to the guy next to them. Maybe twenty or thirty seconds if they believe it matters. After that it's "tell the foreman at break."

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What about language on your sites?

**Marcus:** It varies a lot by region. Texas and Georgia, a big share of the crews are Spanish-first, and on some trades there's a meaningful number of workers whose English is limited. On those sites the Spanish-speaking foremen become the channel, informally. The written forms are in English, and I'll be blunt, a lot of our reporting pipeline assumes English. That's a gap. It's not the headline of my week, but it's a gap.

**Interviewer (C5 probe 2):** What's the longest you think they'd spend before they'd "just deal with it later"?

**Marcus:** Thirty seconds is generous. If there's a login, forget it. The one app that we got some traction on had a QR code on every stair tower, and even that, you had to take your glove off.

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

**Marcus:** Typical path: worker tells foreman. Foreman either fixes it, or tells his company's safety rep, or tells a GC coordinator. The GC coordinator puts it in Procore and assigns it to a company. That company's superintendent assigns it to a foreman. The foreman fixes it, somebody closes it in Procore. If it's SIF-potential, and somebody recognizes that, it also gets entered in the enterprise system and it triggers a precursor review.

Where it gets lost. First hop, worker to foreman, it's verbal and a lot of it stays there. Second, the "which company owns this" question. The GC coordinator has to know whose toe board it is, and on a site with 40 subs, they guess wrong a meaningful amount of the time. Then it bounces. Third, categorization. If the coordinator codes it as housekeeping, it never gets SIF eyes. Fourth, the handoff between Procore and the enterprise system is manual re-entry. People have to type the same thing twice, so they don't.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** How common are duplicates or missing location and photo?

**Marcus:** Duplicates, very common on the big hazards. Three trades see the same open penetration, you might get three entries or zero. Nobody knows which is worse for my data. Location, probably half of what I see is too vague to walk to without calling someone. Photos, better on the GC side, worse on the sub side, and again restricted in some buildings.

**Interviewer (C6 probe 2):** Who decides who owns it?

**Marcus:** The GC coordinator, in practice, often a 26-year-old with a CHST who's been on site four months. We put a lot on them.

**Interviewer (C7):** How do you and your teams currently sort incoming observations to decide what's urgent and who owns it? How long does that take on a typical day?

**Marcus:** At the site level, my site safety managers and their coordinators do the daily triage. From what they tell me, it's one to two hours a day per site for the safety manager, more for coordinators, and Mondays are worse because the weekend stuff and the paper cards come in. At my level, I personally look at SIF-potential items. Across the portfolio that's fifteen to twenty-five a week. I spend three or four hours a week reading them, and more in the week before the quarterly SIF review.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What information do you need to decide?

**Marcus:** What's the energy source, how much of it, and was there a direct control in place. That's the SIF lens: high energy, absence of a direct control. Plus location precise enough to know what's around it, which contractor created it, which one found it, and whether it's still open. Most reports I get have maybe two of those.

**Interviewer (C7 probe 2):** What do you get wrong most often? What do you rebuild by hand?

**Marcus:** Severity consistency across sites. One safety manager calls something high potential, another calls the same scenario medium. That breaks my roll-up and it breaks the owner's trust when they compare campuses. We have a corporate classification guide, it's fourteen pages, and the field uses it the way you'd expect people to use a fourteen-page guide. What we rebuild by hand is the monthly PowerBI deck, every month, basically from scratch.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or instruction was passed along *automatically* or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Marcus:** The camera pilot I mentioned is the best example. It auto-generated "violations," a guy without safety glasses, that kind of thing, and it pushed them to the sub's foreman based on badge data. The badge data was stale. It kept sending an insulation contractor's violations to a fire protection contractor because they'd been on that level two weeks before. The fire protection foreman got something like thirty notifications in a week that weren't his. By the end of that week his crew was calling it the "snitch camera," and he'd set up an email rule to send it to a folder.

Second example, on our own system. Corporate once configured the enterprise EHS platform to auto-notify the project director on every first aid. Within two months the PDs had all filtered those out, so when a real one came through, nobody saw it for a day. Notification fatigue is a design choice.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore an automatically routed item?

**Marcus:** Trust: somebody accountable touched it before it went out. The reason it was routed that way is visible. And there's a measured error rate. I want to know how often it's wrong, not be told it's always right. Ignore: if it's wrong three times in a row, people ignore it forever. There's no recovering from that on a jobsite. And if it undercalls severity on a SIF precursor, that's not a nuisance, that's a liability I'm carrying.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Marcus:** On a data center, the big one is commissioning versus construction. Two weeks ago in Virginia, the commissioning agent had a Level 4 functional test scheduled on switchgear in an electrical room, which meant the room was going energized, and the drywall and paint crews were still finishing in the corridor next to it with a door they needed propped for material. The LOTO boundary and the construction work area overlapped. It was discovered by an electrical GF at the 6:30 foremen's meeting who happened to ask. Resolved by pushing the test four hours and pulling the paint crew out. Nobody got hurt. It cost a half shift for the commissioning team.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** How often does that happen in a week?

**Marcus:** At my level I hear about one or two a month per campus that rise to a real resequencing. At the site level, I'd guess a couple a week are caught at the morning meeting. And I don't know how many aren't caught. My site safety managers or the CxA would give you a better number than I would.

**Interviewer (C9 probe 2):** Who "owned" that conflict?

**Marcus:** On paper, the GC's area superintendent and the commissioning manager jointly. In practice, the GF who asked the question. That's the heroes-over-systems problem. I want systems over heroes.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Marcus:** The biggest was the regional stand-down after the Ohio dropped object. Every project in my region stopped for a two-hour stand-down on the same morning, and Ohio itself had the affected area shut for about three days while we audited toe boards and dropped-object controls at every elevated work area.

Leading up to it, as I said, the hazard was known for four days by at least three people, verbally.

Cost. Across the region the stand-down was roughly 7,000 craft on the clock for two hours, so on the order of 14,000 craft-hours. At a fully loaded blended rate in the neighborhood of $110 an hour, that's around a million and a half dollars of labor, before you count lost productivity after, which is real. The Ohio area shutdown didn't hit the critical path, we got lucky on float. If it had been in the electrical rooms, it would have.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** How is that cost tracked, if at all? LDs?

**Marcus:** Honestly, poorly. The stand-down got its own charge code because I insisted. The three-day area shutdown did not. It went into general conditions and whatever productivity loss the subs claimed. We don't have a cost code for "safety-caused delay," and I'm not sure project controls would want one, because then it's visible. LDs are between us and the owner and they're tied to capacity milestones. I've never seen an LD event attributed to a specific safety hazard in a way anyone would put in writing. So if you ask me to prove a tool reduced stop-work hours, I'd have to build the baseline first. I don't have it today.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, for example one company's crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Marcus:** Closed: the GC coordinator assigns it to the creating contractor, their super assigns a foreman, they fix it, and someone flips the status in Procore. Proves it's closed: in the best case, a closure photo from the fixing contractor and a coordinator who verifies it on their next walk. In the worst case, nobody. About a fifth of those, as I said, have no photo. And verification by the GC is inconsistent, it depends on the coordinator's workload.

The reporter's company almost never gets told. And the owner doesn't see any of it unless it rolls up into the monthly.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help or don't?

**Marcus:** Procore helps within a project. It doesn't talk to the enterprise system without manual work, and it doesn't carry over across projects. The enterprise system is solid for incidents but nobody uses it for observations. The prequal platforms, Avetta, ISN, that type, tell me whether a sub has a safety program on paper, not whether they closed my handrail. None of them is built around "this was found by company A, created by company B, and verified closed by company C."

**Interviewer (C11 probe 2):** What would an owner or a lawyer ask to see?

**Marcus:** Who knew, what they knew, when they knew it, who was told, what was done, and who verified. With timestamps. And it has to be retrievable three years later after the project's demobilized and Procore's archived. After the Ohio event, our legal team asked me for exactly that timeline and I built it from interviews, not records. That's the thing I think about. If that strut had hit the laborer, I'd have been sitting in a deposition explaining a four-day gap with no paper.

I'll add the flip side, because legal will raise it. A record that shows a hazard was known and sat open for four days is also discoverable. So the answer can't be "record less." It has to be "close faster and prove it."

**Interviewer (C12):** How are tools for the site's safety or field coordination paid for today? Whose budget, per what unit (worker, seat, project, month), and who has to sign?

**Marcus:** Three buckets. Enterprise tools, like the EHS system and our Procore enterprise agreement, are corporate. Corporate EHS and IT own them. Those are multi-year, and I don't touch those contracts. Project-level tools come out of the project's HSE line in general conditions. On most of our data center jobs, that's cost-reimbursable under a GMP, so the owner effectively pays and the owner's rep has to be okay with it. That's actually an important point for you: on a lot of these jobs, the owner's view matters as much as mine. Third, the odd pilot comes from my regional budget.

Units: SafetyCulture was per seat, and we bought seats only for GC staff because per-seat for craft was absurd. The camera pilot was per camera per month. The wearable pilot was per device.

Sign-off: I can approve up to $100,000 per project without going to my VP. Above that, my VP signs. Anything that's going to be used across all the projects, or connects to corporate systems, goes through my VP, IT security and procurement, regardless of the dollar amount.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What happens when crews turn over weekly?

**Marcus:** Per-seat licensing falls apart. On Virginia we'll onboard 150 to 300 new workers in a heavy week and lose a similar number. Nobody's going to provision and deprovision licenses at that rate. And if a license means an account, a password, a login, it's dead on arrival for craft.

**Interviewer (C12 probe 2):** What was the last safety-related purchase and how long did approval take?

**Marcus:** An electronic permit-to-work module for one campus, earlier this year. About $38,000 for the project term. I could sign it myself, dollar-wise. It still took seven weeks, because IT security needed the SOC 2 report, SSO integration, and a data retention answer, and procurement needed the vendor on our standard MSA. The wearable pilot the year before was a regional pilot that touched corporate systems, and that took about five months end to end. Most of the five months was IT and legal, not me.

## Section 3 — Concept Presentation & Willingness-to-Pay

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'm going to read a short description of a concept and then ask for your reactions. "Some teams are testing a tool where any worker can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items. The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Marcus:** Okay. Before you ask your question, my question: what's the week-12 number?

**Interviewer (Concept follow-up):** I don't have data to share in this conversation. I'm more interested in how you'd react to it as described.

**Marcus:** Fair enough. That's what they all say, though. Go ahead.

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Marcus:** If it worked as described, and that's a big if, three things. One, the "suggests a category and severity, human confirms" piece is the part I'm actually interested in, more than the voice piece. **Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you, or your crews, refuse to use it?

**Marcus:** I'll give you my list, it's the same list I give everyone.

Week 12. Adoption after novelty. I've watched it drop to 15 percent.

The human reviewer. Who is that, physically? On a site with 2,800 craft on two shifts, is it my coordinator at 2 a.m.? If the reviewer is a bottleneck, it's a queue with a nicer front end. Photo restrictions. On a hyperscale site, the owner may simply not allow it in live buildings. The owner's security team would need to approve where data is stored and who sees images.

Data ownership and retention. When the project demobs, where does the record go, and can I get it back in three years for litigation?

Labor. On the union-heavy sites, if the stewards think this is surveillance or feeds discipline, it's dead. I watched that happen with the cameras. The reporter identity question needs a clear answer before it goes near a labor-management committee.
