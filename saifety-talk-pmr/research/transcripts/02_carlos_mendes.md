**Interviewer (W1 probe):** When you say chasing closure, what did that look like yesterday specifically?

**Carlos:** I had fourteen items on my list that were past seven days open. That's the number the owner looks at. Okay, send me a picture. Then nothing for three hours. One of them sent me a picture of a covered hole that wasn't the right hole. I could tell because the paint marking on the floor was different. **Interviewer (W2):** Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day, other trades, other contractors or other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

**Carlos:** Everybody. It's 40-something subs right now, I think 43 active this week, and about 1,400 craft on days and nights combined. I've got six coordinators, each one has a building or an area plus some subs they're the lead on. Then the GC's superintendents, the general super, our project director, the owner's HSE team, the owner's commissioning people because they're starting to take buildings over in phases, and the owner's equipment vendor, who has their own installation crews, a lot of them Korean technicians, on the electrode and formation lines.

How it actually happens: the 6:00 meeting, radio, WhatsApp, Teams, walking up to people. The formal stuff goes in Procore. The real stuff happens on WhatsApp and face to face, and then I spend Monday morning trying to get the real stuff into the formal stuff.

**Interviewer (W2 probe):** Can you give me an example of the "real stuff" from this week that didn't go through a formal channel first?

**Carlos:** Wednesday the electrical sub's safety rep posts a photo in WhatsApp, a spider box sitting in standing water after the rain, with a cord running out of it across a walkway. No location, no caption, just the photo and "fyi." I had to call him to find out it was the laydown area behind Building 3. By the time I got someone there, somebody had already moved it. So it was fixed. But it never got logged, and if somebody had gotten hit, the only record would've been a WhatsApp photo with no location on some guy's personal phone.

**Interviewer (W3):** When something on site looks "off" to you or your crew, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

**Carlos:** For me personally, if it's life-safety, I stop it right there. I've got stop-work authority and so does everybody on site, that's in orientation, and I try to model it. Tuesday I was walking the Building 2 mezzanine and there was an ironworker tied off to a handrail that's not rated as an anchor. I stopped him, got his foreman, we found him a proper anchor, and it was done in ten minutes. Then I wrote it up in Procore as an observation, assigned it to the steel erector, and brought it up at the weekly sub safety meeting.

If it's not life-safety, so housekeeping, a missing sign, a barricade that's sagging, I snap a photo, and either I fix it on the spot, or it goes in Procore, or it goes on my list for the weekly meeting. I batch the non-urgent stuff. I have to, otherwise I'd never do anything else.

**Interviewer (W3 probe):** And when it's someone on a crew who notices it, not you, what's the most recent example you know of?

**Carlos:** The honest answer is I usually find out after the fact. Same week, a laborer mentioned to one of my coordinators at lunch that the stair tower on the east side of Building 2 had a loose tread "since last week." Since last week. He'd told his foreman. His foreman probably told somebody. Nobody wrote it up. We fixed it that afternoon. But that's the pattern: the crew notices it, it goes one level up verbally, and then it evaporates.

**Interviewer (W4):** What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

**Carlos:** Procore, mainly the Observations module. I like that it's the system of record. The owner can see it, corporate can see it, it's where an auditor looks. What I don't like: most of the craft will never touch it. The subs that have Procore accounts use it, maybe half of them well. Small subs, the three-guy firestopping outfit, don't have accounts, so I'm emailing them. And the notifications go to whoever's on the sub's distribution list, which is often a PM in an office in Atlanta.

SafetyCulture, iAuditor, for inspections. My coordinators love it for scaffold inspections, crane inspections, the weekly area audits. Fast, good templates, photos embedded. But it doesn't talk to Procore, at least not the way we have it set up, so a failed inspection item lives in iAuditor and I have to re-enter it in Procore if I want it tracked there.

Excel is my open-items tracker. I rebuild it every Monday. I hate it and I can't live without it.

WhatsApp: fast, everybody's on it, photos. No structure, no location, no ownership, and legally it scares me a little because it's on personal phones.

Radio for real-time. Teams and Outlook for corporate and the owner. And paper observation cards, which the subs are required to turn in, and which I'll be honest are about 80% garbage.

**Interviewer (W4 probe):** What makes the cards garbage?

**Carlos:** Quotas. We, meaning the GC, set a requirement: each sub foreman turns in a minimum number of observations per week. So Friday afternoon a foreman sits in his truck and writes eight cards that say "Observed worker wearing safety glasses. Positive." That's not an observation. That's a signature on a form. Every once in a while there's a real one in there, and it's three days old by the time it's scanned.

**Interviewer (W5):** How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

**Carlos:** Night coordinator texts me. We have a rule: anything with an injury, a near-miss with serious potential, damage, or a stop-work, he calls me, doesn't text. Everything else he texts, and I read it at 5:20 in the truck. Then there's the WhatsApp overnight. And the superintendents' daily reports in Procore, which are about production, not safety, but sometimes there's a line in there like "lost two hours waiting on the lift" and I go, why?

What I don't find out is what the crews saw and didn't say. That's the gap. On a night like Thursday, I've got maybe 450 people on site and one coordinator. He can't see everything.

## Section 2 — Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your crew or site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Carlos:** The loose stair tread I mentioned is one. A bigger one, about three weeks ago. A telehandler, a lull, from the steel sub was picking material up onto the Building 2 mezzanine, and the load path went over a walkway the electrical crews were using to get to their gang boxes. No barricade, no spotter controlling the walkway. This went on for two days. I found out at the weekly sub safety meeting because the electrical sub's safety rep mentioned it almost as a complaint about the steel sub. Like, "and by the way, their lull was swinging bundles over my guys for two days." Two days. I have six coordinators and nobody on my team saw it, and forty-some electricians walked under it.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why do you think none of the electricians wrote it up?

**Carlos:** A couple of reasons. One, they told their foreman, and their foreman went and talked to the steel foreman, and the steel foreman said, "We're almost done, give us till tomorrow." So in the electrician's mind, it was handled. Two, writing it up means a card or Procore, and the card is a joke and they don't have Procore. Three, and this is the one nobody says out loud, if you write up another sub's crew by name, you're starting a fight between two companies, and the foreman doesn't want his name on that.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** What would have happened to them if they had written it up?

**Carlos:** Officially? Nothing. We have a no-retaliation policy, it's in the site safety plan, I say it at every orientation. Realistically, the electrical foreman would've gotten a call from his own GF saying "Why are you starting stuff with the steel guys? We need their lift next week." There's a lot of horse-trading on a site like this. Lifts, laydown space, access. Nobody wants to burn a favor over a card.

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Carlos:** Good and bad. Good one first, because I want to be fair. A young apprentice pipefitter, maybe second year, stopped a hot-work job last spring because the fire watch had walked off. His own foreman was running the job. He called it, and his foreman was mad for about an hour. I found out, I went and found the kid, thanked him in front of his crew, and we brought it up at the all-hands without his name but everybody knew. That's how it's supposed to work.

Bad one. Earlier this year, a laborer from one of the open-shop concrete subs reported, verbally to one of my coordinators, that his company was stripping forms at an open edge on the second floor of the utility building without guardrails, and his foreman was telling them to just be careful. We went up, we stopped it. Two weeks later, that laborer was gone. "End of assignment." I asked the sub's superintendent directly. He said the pour schedule changed, they didn't need as many laborers. Could be true. I couldn't prove otherwise. But the other laborers on that crew sure drew a conclusion, and I haven't gotten a single report out of that sub's crews since. Not one.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Who heard about it, that he'd been the one to report?

**Carlos:** Everybody. That's the problem. My coordinator went up there with him basically walking beside him. We didn't think about it. On a site this size you think you're anonymous, but a crew of eight guys knows exactly who talked to the safety guy at lunch. I changed how my coordinators handle that now. We try to "discover" things on our own walk. But that only works if we can get there fast.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Has anything like that affected someone's assignments or reputation other than that case?

**Carlos:** Reputation, all the time. There's a word for it on every site: "that guy." That guy who's always calling safety. Sub superintendents don't want that guy on their crew. I've had a sub superintendent tell me, half joking, "Stop sending your guys to talk to my guys, it's slowing me down." That's the culture you're working against. It's not the craft that doesn't care. It's that the incentives around them punish the guy who speaks.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard you or your crew reported in the last month. What did you see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Carlos:** The non-rated anchor on Tuesday I closed myself, that one's easy. A better one: about four weeks ago I logged in Procore a damaged GFCI on a temporary power panel in Building 3, cracked housing, assigned to the electrical sub. Three days later it's marked closed. No photo. I made photos mandatory on close in our Procore setup, but you can attach any photo. They attached a picture of a panel. I walked by the following week and the cracked one was still there. Different panel. So I reopened it, and it took another four days. So seven days total for a GFCI, and it was only really closed because I happened to walk past.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What's the longest you've waited on something like that?

**Carlos:** Formally open, I've got a couple sitting at 30-plus days right now. One is a permanent guardrail detail at a roof hatch that's become a design question between the owner and the architect. Nobody's in danger because we have a temporary rail and a lock on the hatch, but it sits on my open-items list and the owner auditor asks about it every month. The ones that bother me aren't the long formal ones. They're the ones that got closed on paper but not in the field.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** And for the person on a crew who reported something, what do they typically hear back?

**Carlos:** Nothing. I'll be honest. If a crew member tells his foreman, and his foreman tells the sub safety rep, and it goes in WhatsApp, and my coordinator gets it into Procore, and it gets fixed, nobody goes back and tells the guy who saw it. There's no path back to him. He sees it fixed, maybe, if he goes back to that area. If he doesn't, he assumes nothing happened. And next time he doesn't bother.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report: cards, quotas, rewards, apps? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Carlos:** All of it. On this job: the card quota, which I've described. It "works" in the sense that we get a big number every week, and the number goes in the owner report. It doesn't work in any sense I care about.

Rewards: we do a monthly drawing. Turn in a quality observation, you get a ticket, there's a Yeti cooler, a gift card, once a set of tires. First month, great. Second month, the subs figured out that the foreman could write the card and put the crew's names on it. By month three, it's just more cards.

On the tech side, I've personally piloted three things on this job and the one before, and I'll tell you which ones died because I'm not precious about it. We tried SafetyCulture for crew-level reporting, not just inspections. It needed accounts. With 1,400 people and something like 150 new orientations a week at peak, account management was a full-time job, and a foreman is not going to create a login in the rain. Died in maybe six weeks for crews. We still use it for inspections, where the users are my coordinators and it's great.

Second was a computer-vision camera vendor. Cameras on the crane areas and the laydown yard, flagging PPE and exclusion-zone stuff. 90-day pilot, free. It generated, I'm not exaggerating, a couple hundred alerts a day, most of them "hard hat not detected" because a guy took it off to wipe his forehead in 95-degree heat. My coordinators stopped opening the alerts in about two weeks. Then the union steward for one of the core trades asked, very reasonably, whether the footage was going to be used for discipline, and nobody on the vendor side could give a straight answer. We ended it around day 60.

Third was a lone-worker wearable for night shift and some confined-space work. The device was fine. The charging was the problem. Somebody has to collect them, charge them, hand them out, track who has which one. Nobody owned that. It faded out.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** What did people say about those in the trailer or the break room?

**Carlos:** The cameras, the craft called it "the snitch cam." That name spread in about three days. The SafetyCulture thing, people just said "another login." Honestly the most useful feedback I ever got was from a GF who said, "Carlos, if it takes longer than calling you, I'm calling you." And he's right. The benchmark isn't the old card. The benchmark is him calling me on the radio.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why do you think the card quota has stuck when the others faded?

**Carlos:** Because it's required and it produces a number corporate and the owner want to see. It sticks because it's a reporting requirement, not because it works. That's exactly what I don't want to add more of.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment you notice a hazard mid-task: where are you, what's in your hands, what's around you, and how much time do you realistically have to tell someone?

**Carlos:** For me, I've got a tablet and a phone, I'm walking, I'm not on tools, so I have time. I'm the easy case. I'll spend five minutes on a write-up if I have to.

For the crews, completely different. A fitter on a scissor lift 20 feet up, gloves on, both hands on a pipe, hearing protection in because they're running the chillers in the next bay. Phones: on this site, personal phones are allowed in most construction areas but not while operating equipment, and in the areas we've turned over to the owner, some of the dry-room spaces, it's gowning rules and phones are restricted or have to be bagged. So realistically, a crew member notices something, and he's going to deal with it at break, or at lunch, or he's going to yell to his foreman.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What's the longest you think a crew member would spend before they'd "just deal with it later"?

**Carlos:** Thirty seconds, max. Honestly less for a lot of them. If it's longer than it takes to send a text to his foreman, it becomes an end-of-day thing, and end-of-day things don't happen. They're tired, they're in the truck, they want to get to the hotel.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** You mentioned language earlier. How does that play in?

**Carlos:** A lot of our crews are Spanish-first. I'd guess 40%, maybe more on some of the open-shop subs. My Spanish is job-site Spanish, I get by. Two of my coordinators are fluent, the other four aren't. Then the owner's equipment vendor has Korean technicians installing process equipment, and they're working right next to our subs, and there's basically no shared language there except pointing. I've seen a Korean tech and an electrician have a 10-minute argument about a LOTO lock using hand signals. The paper cards are in English and Spanish. The Korean techs don't fill out our cards at all. They're not our sub, technically, they're the owner's vendor.

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

**Carlos:** Typical path: crew member sees it, tells his foreman. Foreman either fixes it, which is great and never gets recorded, or tells his sub's safety rep. Safety rep posts it in WhatsApp, maybe with a photo. One of my coordinators picks it up, figures out where it is and whose it is, puts it in Procore as an observation, assigns it to the responsible sub. Procore emails the sub's distribution list. Someone at the sub, hopefully the super, sees it and tells a foreman to fix it. Foreman fixes it. Someone at the sub closes it in Procore with a photo. My coordinator, in theory, verifies.

Where it breaks: first, location. "Level 2 by the column" in a building that's over a million square feet is not a location. My coordinators spend a lot of time just figuring out where something is.

Second, ownership. Who created it? If the electricians report an open penetration, it's probably not theirs, it's the mechanical sub's or the firestopping sub's. My coordinator has to figure that out, and gets it wrong maybe one in five times, and then it bounces for two days while two subs argue about whose hole it is.

Third, duplicates. The same open penetration gets reported by three different trades through three different safety reps. Now I've got three WhatsApp photos, maybe two Procore observations, and on Monday I'm deduping them in Excel.

Fourth, the email goes to the wrong person. A PM in the office, not the field super.

Fifth, nights. Something reported on night shift waits until my day coordinators pick it up at 6 a.m., and by then the night crew is gone and nobody can tell you exactly where it was.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Who decides who owns it?

**Carlos:** My coordinators, and then me if it's disputed. That's a judgment call based on knowing the site: who's working in that area, whose scope it is, who had that lift last. It's not something you can look up. It's in people's heads.

**Interviewer (C7):** How do you currently sort incoming observations to decide what's urgent and who owns it? How long does that take on a typical day?

**Carlos:** Morning is the big block. 5:20 to about 6:00 I'm going through overnight myself, maybe 30 to 40 minutes. Then each coordinator does their area, probably 30 to 45 minutes each in the morning, and then it trickles all day. Add it up, my team probably spends five or six person-hours a day just sorting and routing, before anybody fixes anything. And then Monday, the Excel rebuild, that's three to four hours for me, pulling Procore exports, the iAuditor failed items, the scanned cards, the WhatsApp stuff I flagged, into one list so I can tell the PD and the owner what's open.

I sort by one question first: is somebody exposed right now? If yes, it's a phone call and a coordinator goes. If no, what's the potential severity, fall, energy, struck-by, crane, confined space, those go to the top. Everything else is batched.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What information do you need to decide, and what's usually missing?

**Carlos:** Where exactly, what exactly, a photo, is anyone exposed right now, and who's working there. The photo is usually there. Location is usually missing or vague. "Is anyone exposed right now" is almost never answered. I have to call.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What do you get wrong most often?

**Carlos:** Ownership, like I said. And severity on stuff that looks minor in a photo. A photo of a cord across a walkway looks like housekeeping. The same photo, if you know that cord's feeding a welder and there's standing water, is a different story. The photo doesn't tell you that. The guy who took it knows.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or instruction was passed along *automatically* or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Carlos:** Procore's auto-notifications are the everyday example. An observation assigned to a sub automatically emails their distribution list. Most sub field supers have filtered those emails into a folder. I know because two of them told me. So "automatic" in practice means "ignored."

The camera alerts are the other one. Automatic, high volume, mostly wrong, and my team learned to ignore them in two weeks. And once people learn to ignore a channel, you don't get them back. Even when it's right, they don't look.

And one more, the owner's system pushed an automated heat-illness advisory to all site badges' registered emails, well, it went to all the sub PMs. Every day in July. By the second week, nobody read it, including on the one day it actually mattered, when the heat index went over 110.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore an automatically routed item?

**Carlos:** Trust: if it's right almost every time, if I can see *why* it went where it went, and if a person I know touched it before it reached the sub. If one of my coordinators looked at it, I trust it. Ignore: if it's wrong twice in a week. That's about the tolerance. And if it's noisy. Volume kills trust faster than errors do.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Carlos:** Ten days ago. The owner's commissioning team was starting to energize a section of the formation building, part of a phased turnover, and they'd put their locks on a distribution panel. Our electrical sub still had punch-list terminations on circuits fed from that same panel. Nobody had connected the commissioning plan to our electrical sub's three-week lookahead. The electrical foreman found out when his crew showed up and there were owner locks on the panel they needed. Good news, the LOTO worked. Bad news, eight electricians stood around for about half a day, and the foreman was frustrated enough that he talked about "just working around it," which is exactly the conversation I never want to hear near energized equipment.

It got resolved the next morning at the 6:00 meeting, with the owner's commissioning lead, our electrical super and me in a room.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** How often does something like that happen in a week?

**Carlos:** Something that costs real time, a crew standing down or a re-sequence, maybe two or three a week now that turnover's started. Minor stuff, a lift in someone's way, two crews wanting the same laydown, daily. As the owner takes over more buildings it's getting worse, because now there are two sets of rules, ours and theirs, in the same building.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** Who "owned" that conflict?

**Carlos:** Nobody, until it was a conflict. After that, me, kind of by default. That's the honest answer. It's not really a safety problem until it becomes one, and then it lands on safety.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Carlos:** Five weeks ago, a dropped object. A three-foot piece of unistrut fell from the Building 2 mezzanine, about 25 feet, and landed maybe ten feet from a laborer. No injury. Serious potential, obviously. We did a site-wide stand-down, all crews, about 90 minutes, to go over dropped-object prevention, toe boards, tool tethering, housekeeping at edges.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Was the hazard known beforehand? By whom?

**Carlos:** Yes, and that's the part that keeps me up. One of my coordinators had logged it in an iAuditor area inspection nine days before: "Housekeeping at mezzanine edge, loose material, toe board missing along gridline C." Assigned to the sub. The sub had it marked "in progress." It was in the system. For nine days.

I've been on a site where that exact thing ended with somebody dead. Houston, a missing guardrail, and the investigation found three guys had noticed it days before. I'm not going to be the safety manager who has to sit in a deposition and explain why it was in the system and not closed. That's my actual nightmare, more than the OSHA citation.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** How is the cost of that stand-down tracked, if at all?

**Carlos:** Not well. Project controls sees it as labor hours with a stand-down cost code, if the subs use the code, which they mostly don't. I did my own math for the PD afterward: roughly 1,400 people times an hour and a half, call it 2,000 labor hours, at a blended loaded rate of maybe $80 to $90 an hour, you're somewhere around $170,000 to $180,000 just in the stand-down. That doesn't count the ramp back up, and it doesn't count the mezzanine being shut down for most of a day while we did the fix.

On LDs, no, it didn't hit an LD. Our contract has milestone LDs on mechanical completion by building, and a 90-minute stand-down doesn't move that by itself. But three or four of those in a quarter and our general super starts looking at me sideways. The PD cared about the $170,000 number. I'm not sure the owner ever saw it.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, for example your crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Carlos:** On paper, my coordinator assigns it in Procore to the sub that created it, the sub fixes it and closes it with a photo, my coordinator verifies. In practice, the verification step is where it falls apart, because my coordinators have 200 or 300 open items between them at any given time. They verify the high-severity ones. The rest, we trust the photo, and I told you how that went with the GFCI.

Who proves it's closed? Right now, me, with my Monday spreadsheet and a Procore export. If that sub doesn't have a Procore account, it's an email chain.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help or don't?

**Carlos:** Procore helps, because it's the record. It doesn't help with the crews, because they're not in it, and it doesn't help across the owner line, because the owner's commissioning team and their vendors are in their own system. iAuditor helps with inspections but it's a separate island. WhatsApp helps with speed and hurts with everything else. None of them close the loop back to the person who saw it.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** What would an owner or lawyer ask to see?

**Carlos:** I can answer that one from experience, because of the citation we're appealing. It was a fall protection item on a sub's crew. Our corporate counsel asked for every observation, inspection, and communication about that area for the 60 days before the citation. It took me and one coordinator about two weeks to assemble it. Procore exports, iAuditor PDFs, scanned cards, and then the WhatsApp messages, which meant asking sub safety reps to export chats from their personal phones. That was uncomfortable for everybody.

What the lawyer wanted to know was simple: when did we know, who did we tell, what did they do, when did we verify it was fixed. Four questions. And it took two weeks to answer them because the answers were in five places.

The owner auditor asks a lighter version of the same thing every month: open items over seven days, by sub, and show me the close-out photos for a sample.

**Interviewer (C12):** How are tools for the site's safety or field coordination paid for today? Whose budget, per what unit (worker, seat, project, month), and who has to sign?

**Carlos:** Couple of buckets. Procore is enterprise, corporate pays for it, it's not on my budget and I don't know the number. SafetyCulture we pay per seat at the project level, maybe 12 seats for my team and a few supers, comes out of the project's safety line in general conditions. PPE, signage, the monthly drawing prizes, training, all of that is project safety budget.

My own discretionary is about $15,000 a quarter. I can spend that without asking anybody. Above that, it goes to the PD. If it's software, it also goes to corporate IT for a security review, and if it's anything that touches worker data, corporate EHS wants a say, and probably legal.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What was the last safety-related purchase, and how long did approval take?

**Carlos:** Last one I did myself: 20 four-gas monitors for confined-space work, about $12,000. Out of my discretionary, ordered in two days, here in a week.

Last one I had to take up: a heat-stress program this spring, cooling stations plus some monitoring gear, about $40,000. PD approved it in maybe three weeks, mostly because I brought him the heat-illness incident from last year and a number. And the camera pilot, which was free for 90 days, still had to go through corporate IT security. That took almost three months before we could turn it on. The pilot almost ended before it started.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What happens with per-seat or per-worker tools when crews turn over weekly?

**Carlos:** It's a mess. We orient 100, 150 new people a week at peak and lose almost as many. Any tool that needs a named account per worker, somebody has to add and remove people every week, and that somebody ends up being my admin or one of my coordinators. That's part of why the SafetyCulture crew rollout died. For seat-based stuff, we only ever license the permanent people: my team, supers, a few sub safety reps.

## Section 3 — Concept Presentation & Willingness-to-Pay

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'm going to read a short description of a concept, and then ask for your reactions. I'll read it exactly as written.

"Some teams are testing a tool where any worker can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items. The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Carlos:** Okay. Can you read the second sentence again?

**Interviewer (Concept):** "The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner."

**Carlos:** Okay. Go ahead.

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Carlos:** If it did what that says, which is a big if, the thing it could change is my Monday. The one queue. If everything that currently comes in through WhatsApp, cards and Procore came into one place with a location and a photo, I don't rebuild the spreadsheet. That's three or four hours a week for me, and more for my coordinators.

The reporter getting notified on close, that's the part I'd actually care about most, and I don't think it's obvious to people who haven't done this job. That's the thing that's missing. The guy never hears back. If he heard back, he'd report again. I believe that, but I've never been able to test it, because we've never had a way to tell him.

Voice in their own language: that could open up the Spanish-first crews, and maybe the vendor techs, but I'd want to see it work with a real crew, in the noise, before I believe it.

What it wouldn't change: my coordinators still have to walk. It doesn't fix the sub superintendent who hides stuff; he'll just close things faster with bad photos. It doesn't fix the horse-trading between subs. It doesn't fix the retaliation thing on its own, unless the reporter's identity is protected in a way the crews actually believe. And it doesn't replace the weekly sub safety meeting.

And honestly, "a human safety reviewer confirms or edits it." On my site that's my coordinators. So it changes their week too, and not necessarily in a good way. If it's 20 real reports a day, great. If it's 300 "no hard hat" reports a day, it's the camera pilot again.

**Interviewer (P1 probe):** When you say "a big if," what specifically would have to be true?

**Carlos:** That the location is real. That the owner it suggests is right most of the time, because if my coordinators are fixing the owner on every item, it's slower than what they do now. And that it lands in Procore, or pushes to Procore, or whatever the system of record is. Because if it doesn't, it's another inbox, and I've killed tools for being another inbox. The owner audits Procore. My lawyer pulls from Procore. I'm not maintaining two systems of record.

**Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you, or your crew, refuse to use it?

**Carlos:** A list. First, another inbox, which I just said. That's the killer.

Second, discoverability, and this one cuts both ways for me. I want the record. I want to be able to show a lawyer "we knew at 9:14, assigned at 9:40, closed with a photo at 2:15, verified by a coordinator." That's the dream. But the flip side is every item that doesn't close fast is now timestamped, with a crew member's voice on it, in a system someone can subpoena. Corporate legal is going to have a real opinion on that, and I can't answer for them.

Third, the severity suggestion. If the system calls something low that's actually high, and my coordinator just clicks "confirm" because he's got 60 items, and then somebody gets hurt, that's the deposition I'm afraid of. I'd need the reviewer step to be real, not a rubber stamp. And I'd need to know how often it's wrong.

Fourth, "what's the rule here with the source cited." Which rules? OSHA 1926? The owner's HSE requirements, which are stricter than OSHA in places? Our corporate standards? Our site-specific plan? If it cites the wrong one, or an old revision, and a foreman acts on it, that's on us. I'd want to control what it's allowed to cite. I'd honestly consider turning that part off until it proved itself.

Fifth, for the crews: if it needs an app on a personal phone with a login, it's dead. The union trades on this site will not put a company app on a personal phone, and I don't blame them. It'd need to be a QR code, a text number, something with no account. And the stewards will ask the same question they asked about the cameras: can this be used to discipline my members? If the answer isn't a clear no, in writing, they'll tell their people not to touch it.

Sixth, anonymity. If a crew can report anonymously, how does my coordinator ask a follow-up question? And if they can't, you're back to the laborer from the concrete sub. I don't know the right answer there, but whoever builds this has to have thought about it harder than I have.

**Interviewer (P2 probe):** Of those, which would make you personally stop using it?

**Carlos:** Another inbox, and my coordinators ignoring it. If after three or four weeks my coordinators are working around it, I kill it. I've done it before. I don't need corporate to tell me a tool's dead; I can see it in how my people behave.

**Interviewer (P3):** Who on your site would use it most, and who would ignore or resist it?

**Carlos:** Most: my coordinators, if it actually saves them triage. The sub safety reps, especially the good ones, because it replaces WhatsApp and gives them credit. Maybe the younger craft, the apprentices, the travelers who are on their phones anyway. Maybe the Spanish-first crews, if it really works in Spanish and nobody's name goes to their super.

Ignore or resist: sub superintendents, some of them, because it makes their open items visible to me and the owner in a way they can't manage. They'll say it's slowing them down. The general superintendent, who'll see it as more safety noise until it saves him a stoppage. Union stewards, until they get the discipline question answered. Corporate IT, because it's another vendor. And the older GFs, who are going to keep calling me on the radio, and honestly that's fine.

**Interviewer (P3 probe):** Who would decide whether it gets used across all the subs?

**Carlos:** The PD can require it through our subcontract safety requirements, the same way we require the cards. That's the lever. But requiring it and people using it are two different things. I've seen that movie with the cards.

**Interviewer (P5):** Imagine two ways to pay: (a) a project-level subscription at **$150,000 per project-year** covering everyone on site, all employers included, or (b) traditional per-seat licensing at about **$10 per user per month**. How would each land with you, and why?

**Carlos:** Neither one is my money, first of all. $150,000 is ten quarters of my discretionary. So that's a PD conversation and probably corporate EHS, and corporate IT gets a vote no matter what.

On the $150,000: on a $3.1 billion job, it's not a big number in absolute terms. It's a rounding error on the contract. But it's not competing with the contract; it's competing inside the safety line and the PD's general conditions, where it is a big number. For $150,000 a year I could almost fund another safety coordinator, loaded. So the question the PD will ask me, and the one I'd ask myself, is: is this worth more than a seventh coordinator? And I'd have to prove that with data, not a demo.

The stand-down I told you about was around $170,000 in labor for 90 minutes. If I could show that something like this prevented one of those a year, the math works. But I can't show that before I buy it, and I'm not going to walk into the PD's office and promise it.

Per-seat at $10: I'm doing the math. 1,400 people is about $168,000 a year, which is more than the project price, and it's a nightmare to administer with our turnover, and nobody's paying for the subs' crews on a per-seat basis. So realistically, per-seat means I license my team and a handful of people, maybe 30 seats, which is cheap, about $3,600 a year, and useless, because the whole point is the crews. So per-seat doesn't make sense for this. Project-level is the right structure. Whether $150,000 is the right number, I'm skeptical.

**Interviewer (P5 probe):** What would have to be true for the $150,000 option to be something you'd take to your PD?

**Carlos:** Pilot data from my site, not someone else's. And it'd have to replace something, not stack on top. If it replaced the card program and the Monday spreadsheet and cut my coordinators' triage time in half, I could make the case. If it's on top of Procore and iAuditor and the cards, no. **Interviewer (P5b):** Another version being considered is **$24,000 per project-year**, sized for a ~200-person resident core team. How does that compare?

**Carlos:** That's a very different conversation. $24,000 I could get done with the PD pretty quickly, maybe even partly out of my own budget over two quarters, although my discretionary is already spoken for with gas monitors and training most quarters.
