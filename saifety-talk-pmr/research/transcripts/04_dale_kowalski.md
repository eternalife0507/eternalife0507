Printed lookahead. The P6 three-week. Donna prints it for me every Monday, I mark it up. I like paper because I can write on it. Whiteboard in the trailer for the big milestones. Phone, calls and texts. I can't read the little stuff without my glasses, and I'm not taking my glasses out in the field every five minutes.

My team uses Procore and Bluebeam. The field engineers, the safety guy. I don't. I've got people for that.

**Interviewer (W4 probe):** What do you dislike about Procore, if anything?

**Interviewer (W5):** How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

**Interviewer (W5 probe):** Have you ever found out about something later than you wanted to?

**Dale:** ...Yeah. Everybody has. Usually from the owner, which is the worst way.

**Interviewer (W5 probe):** Can you give me an example?

**Dale:** It'll come up. Keep going.

## Section 2: Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your crew or site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Dale:** That happens a hundred times a day and thank God it does. Wednesday a carpenter saw a missing toe board on a scaffold, put one on, told his foreman. Didn't write it up. Good. That's what I want.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why didn't he write it up?

**Dale:** Because he fixed it. If they've got time to fill out a card, they've got time to fix it.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** What would have happened if he had written it up?

**Dale:** I'll tell you exactly. Card goes to safety. Safety puts it in Procore. The scaffold sub's safety guy gets an email. Now it's an open item. It shows up on the owner's weekly safety report as "scaffold deficiency." And my area super gets asked in the owner meeting Tuesday why there are twelve open scaffold items. For a toe board that was fixed Wednesday morning. That's how a near-miss program turns into a snitch program. Nobody got safer. My super looks bad.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Are there things people notice that they don't fix themselves and also don't write up?

**Dale:** [pause] Sure. Stuff that's not theirs. Stuff above their pay grade. Those are supposed to come to a super.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** And do they?

**Dale:** Most of the time.

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Dale:** On my job? Nothing happens to them. I don't put up with that.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Who hears about it when that happens?

**Dale:** The super. The other trade's foreman. Me, if it's big. The guy's own foreman, usually, 'cause he's standing right there.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Has it ever affected someone's assignments, layoffs or reputation, on this job or a previous one?

**Dale:** [long pause] Not on my crews. I've seen subs move a guy. Hospital job, years back, a fire protection foreman kept calling out the drywallers for blocking his work with open penetrations. The drywall sub complained to his sub, next week he's on another job. Was that about safety? Partly. Partly he was a pain in the ass. There's a difference between a guy who raises a hazard and a guy who's always got a complaint. Everybody on a site knows the difference.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** How do they know the difference?

**Dale:** You just know. Thirty-six years, you know.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard you or your crew reported in the last month. What did you see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Dale:** I don't report. People report to me. When I call something in to a super on the radio, I hear back "done, boss" in twenty minutes, or I go look.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What about things that go through the formal channel, through safety or Procore? What do you hear back on those?

**Dale:** Nothing. That's the honest answer. It goes in the system. The owner's safety rep wrote up a housekeeping thing, cords across a stair in Building 1, maybe a month ago. Sub cleaned it up the same day. It sat open in Procore for three weeks because nobody clicked the button. Showed up on the owner's report every week as open. I heard about that one plenty.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What's the longest you've seen something sit before anyone heard back?

**Dale:** In a system? Months. On an old job we found cards in a box after the job closed out. Nobody ever read them.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report (cards, quotas, rewards, apps)? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Dale:** All of it. Every foreman turns in two observation cards a week. That's the current one, corporate rule. Before that, a phone app on the Ashburn job, 2021, some corporate EHS thing. Pizza lunches for the most cards. A gift card drawing.

The app got, I don't know, three, four thousand cards the first month. Half of them were "good job wearing PPE." By month three it was dead. The cards now? Guys fill them out in the truck at 3:25 on Friday. I've seen it. I've probably signed off on some of them.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why did the app fade?

**Dale:** Because nobody read the cards. Nobody did anything with them. Guys aren't stupid. You make them do homework nobody grades, they stop.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** What did people say about it in the trailer?

**Dale:** "Card or it didn't happen." That was the joke. Or they called it the rat app. The foremen hated it because the owner could see the count by sub, so the subs started competing on card numbers. Paperwork contest.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment you notice a hazard mid-task: where are you, what's in your hands, what's around you, and how much time do you realistically have to tell someone?

**Dale:** Walking. Radio in one hand, coffee in the other half the morning. Lookahead folded in my back pocket. Load banks and generators running in the yard, you can't hear yourself in some of those rooms. How much time? I don't "tell someone." I grab the foreman who's standing there or I key the radio. Five seconds.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What about your phone? Where does that fit?

**Dale:** Pocket. I'm not typing on a phone in the middle of an electrical room. I can't see the thing without glasses. And gloves.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What's the longest you'd spend before you'd "just deal with it later"?

**Dale:** I don't deal with things later. That's the whole job. The ones that bite you are the ones somebody else decided to deal with later.

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

**Dale:** The formal ones? Guy tells his foreman or fills out a card. Card goes to our safety coordinator. She puts it in Procore, picks a sub. Email goes to that sub's safety rep, who might be on site or might be covering three jobs from an office in Richmond. He calls the foreman. Foreman fixes it or doesn't. Somebody's supposed to close it with a photo.

Where does it slow down? The office guy in Richmond. And picking the sub. Half the time the card says "Building 2, level 1, cords" and there are six trades on level 1.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Who decides who owns it?

**Dale:** Safety picks. If they pick wrong, the sub kicks it back, "not ours," and it sits. If it's something real, it comes to a super and the super decides. Really it's the supers who decide. The system's just the record.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Do you see duplicates?

**Dale:** Sure. Three guys write up the same cord. Owner rep writes it up too. Now it's four open items.

**Interviewer (C7):** How do you currently sort incoming observations to decide what's urgent and who owns it? How long does that take on a typical day?

**Dale:** I don't sort observations. Safety does that. I sort radio calls, in my head. Is it going to hurt somebody, is it going to stop work, is it on my critical path. That order. Takes no time. It's what I do.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What information do you need to make that call?

**Dale:** Where it is and who's in there. That's it. If it's in the electrical rooms right now, it's urgent, period.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What do you get wrong most often?

**Dale:** [pause] Who's actually in an area. The lookahead says one thing and there are three other crews in there that weren't on it. Controls guys, low-voltage, somebody's doing punch. Lookahead's wrong by Tuesday.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or instruction was passed along *automatically* or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Dale:** The Ashburn app. It auto-sent every card to the sub's corporate safety guy. So a foreman writes "ladder not tied off" and the mechanical sub's corporate VP of safety in Pittsburgh gets an email, calls the owner, owner calls my PD, PD calls me. Over a ladder. My supers started telling their guys to just come to them instead. Which, fine.

Procore sends emails too. Everybody's got a folder for those. Nobody reads the folder.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore something that was routed automatically?

**Dale:** I'd ignore it. I'd trust a person. If my super tells me, I trust it. If a thing emails me, I don't know who looked at it. Maybe if a person I know looked at it first. Maybe.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Dale:** Friday. Every day, but Friday. Electrical and mechanical both scheduled the one scissor lift in the Building 2 corridor. Nobody knew till both foremen were standing there. Hector called me, I called it: electricians first, they're critical path. Mechanical went and did hangers somewhere else.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** How often does that happen in a week?

**Dale:** Couple times a day. Space, lifts, the crane, the hoist. Hot work next to somebody's fire watch, a lockout on a panel the controls guys need. Big job, one set of rooms, everybody's late, everybody wants in.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** What did it cost in time?

**Dale:** That one? Mechanical lost a morning. Most of them, an hour here, a half-day there. Doesn't hit the critical path usually. Usually.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** Who owned the conflict?

**Dale:** Me. It's always me in the end.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Dale:** [long pause] Alright. May. Building 1 main electrical room. The owner's safety called a stop-work on the whole room and the rooms next to it. Energized panel. We had temp power backfed through a panel that was supposed to be dead and locked out. Some tie-in the electrical sub did for temporary lighting, and it didn't get put on the LOTO log right. A journeyman got his tester on it and it lit up. Nobody got hurt.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Was the hazard known beforehand?

**Dale:** [pause] Yeah. It was known. That's the part. The electricians had been talking about that panel for a week. "Don't trust that one, it's hot." In their gang box, at lunch. The foreman knew. The owner's commissioning agent overheard it in the room and went straight to their safety rep, and she wrote it up and called the stop.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Who else knew?

**Dale:** My area super had heard something. He told me afterward. He'd been told "it's being handled." It wasn't being handled. It didn't get to me.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** What did it cost the schedule?

**Dale:** Four days. Four days of float, gone, on the critical path to energization. Owner did a LOTO audit on the whole site, retraining, the electrical sub had to rewrite their temp power procedure. We got two days back with Saturdays and a second shift on the electricians. The premium time came out of my margin. The other two days we're still carrying.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** How is that cost tracked, if at all?

**Dale:** It's tracked in the schedule. Float's float. The overtime is tracked in the cost report. Nobody writes "safety" next to it. It's just "recovery." LDs, I'm not telling you our number. It's big enough that I think about it every morning.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Looking back, what would you have wanted to happen differently?

**Dale:** It should have come to me. On the radio, day one. I'd have had that panel fixed in an afternoon and nobody would've written anything. Instead I found out from the owner. In writing.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, e.g. your crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Dale:** Super walks it. Sees it's fixed. It's closed. Proof? The super's word. My word.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help or don't, for those?

**Dale:** Radio helps. Procore doesn't, because the guy who closes it isn't the guy who opened it and neither of them's the guy who fixed it. So it sits open, like the cord thing.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** What would an owner or a lawyer ask to see?

**Dale:** A lawyer wants to know who knew what, and when. I've sat in a deposition. 2009, hospital job, a fatality. Not our crew, an elevator sub. They had every piece of paper on that job up on a screen. That's the thing about writing everything down. You've got a thousand cards saying everybody knew something, and then something happens, and the question is why didn't you do anything about card 612. Owner wants to see it's closed. Lawyer wants to see that you knew.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** And what would the owner want to see?

**Dale:** No open items on their report. And no surprises.

**Interviewer (C12):** How are tools for the site's safety or field coordination paid for today? Whose budget, per what unit (worker, seat, project, month), and who has to sign?

**Dale:** Procore's corporate, it gets charged to the job as a line in general conditions. I don't see that number. Safety software is corporate EHS, they pick it, they pay it or they charge it back. Radios, light towers, rental equipment, site stuff, that's mine. I can do about twenty-five grand without going to Rich. Above that, Rich. Big stuff, corporate. The badging and access system is the owner's, they pay per head I think.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What happens with per-person costs when crews turn over weekly?

**Dale:** I get thirty, forty new faces a week in orientation. And that many leave. Anything per-head, somebody's counting heads every month and arguing about the bill. Donna would kill me.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What was the last safety-related purchase, and how long did approval take?

**Dale:** New radios. Sixty Motorolas with better range in the electrical rooms, because the old ones died in there. I signed it the same day. Before that, safety wanted some camera system on the tower cranes, went to corporate, IT got involved, took four months and we still don't have it. Crane's coming down in November.

## Section 3: Concept Presentation and Willingness-to-Pay

**Interviewer (Concept statement):** I'm going to read a short description of something and then ask for your reaction. "Some teams are testing a tool where any worker can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items. The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Dale:** So it's an app. One of those AI things.

**Interviewer (Concept statement):** What does that bring to mind for you?

**Dale:** The Ashburn app. With a robot on it.

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Dale:** My week? Probably nothing. Maybe it changes the safety coordinator's week, she's the one typing cards into Procore. What wouldn't it change? Me. I'm still going to be on the radio and walking it. My supers are still going to fix stuff before anybody types anything.

**Interviewer (P1 probe):** Can you give me an example of where it might show up in your week, even if it doesn't change much?

**Dale:** [pause] If it was the kind of thing where the electricians' "that panel's hot" gets to somebody... That would've been worth something. But it would've gone to the owner's queue. "The owner sees one queue." It'd be the same thing that happened, just faster. It needs to get to my super, not the owner.

**Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you, or your crew, refuse to use it?

**Dale:** The owner sees everything before I do. That's number one. Surprises in writing. Number two, my supers get graded on how many open items they have. That's what happened with the cards. Number three, it pings me. I don't want my phone going off every time somebody sees a cord.

And the lawyer thing. Every one of those is a record. A thousand records.

**Interviewer (P2 probe):** What would make your supers or the crews refuse it?

**Dale:** If it looks like snitching. If a foreman thinks his guys are writing him up to the owner, he'll tell them not to use it and they won't. And if it takes longer than just telling the super.

**Interviewer (P2 probe):** Is there anything in the description that doesn't worry you?

**Dale:** The language part, fine. Half my concrete and drywall guys speak Spanish, and there's some Portuguese. My supers get by. And a person looks at it before it goes out. That's better than Ashburn. The "what's the rule" thing, the safety kids would like that. The rule on my site is what my super says.

**Interviewer (P3):** Who on your site would use it most, and who would ignore or resist it?

**Dale:** Safety would use it. Our safety manager would love it, he loves anything with a dashboard. The owner's safety rep would love it. Some of the young guys, the electricians maybe, they're on their phones anyway.

Ignore it: my supers. The foremen. Me. The old heads. And some of the subs are going to fight it if their name's on an owner queue.

**Interviewer (P3 probe):** Why would the supers ignore it?

**Dale:** Because they're already fixing it. It's one more thing to look at, and the answer's the same: go look, get the foreman, fix it.

**Interviewer (P5):** Imagine two ways to pay: (a) a project-level subscription at $150,000 per project-year covering everyone on site, all employers included, or (b) traditional per-seat licensing at about $10 per user per month. How would each land with you, and why?

Per-seat, ten bucks, I've got eleven hundred guys at peak, that's what, eleven grand a month, a hundred thirty-some a year. And half of those guys are different guys by spring. Who's counting users? Per-seat's worse. At least the project number's one number.

**Interviewer (P5 probe):** When you say no to the $150,000, what's behind that?

**Dale:** It's not mine to spend, it's Rich's, and Rich would ask me if I believe in it, and I'd tell him the truth. I don't. Not yet. And I'm not spending a hundred fifty on something so the owner can see more open items about my job.

**Interviewer (P5 probe):** Is there any situation where a number like that would make sense to you?

**Dale:** [pause] If it kept one stop-work off my critical path. I know what four days costs. It costs more than that. But you can't prove it'll do that, and I'm not paying on a promise.

**Interviewer (P5b):** Another version being considered is $24,000 per project-year, sized for a ~200-person resident core team. How does that compare?

**Dale:** That's under my number. Twenty-five's my line. So I could sign that without calling anybody. Doesn't mean I would.

Two hundred people, who are those? My whole staff's forty-five, maybe fifty. The other hundred and fifty are the subs' foremen and GFs, and they're not mine. And the guys who actually saw the panel were journeymen, they wouldn't be in your two hundred.

**Interviewer (P5b probe):** How does it compare to the first number for you?

**Dale:** Cheaper's better if it doesn't work. If it works, price isn't the conversation. I just don't know that it works. Twenty-four, I'd at least listen. A hundred fifty, the meeting's over.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what annual price per project would this be so cheap you'd doubt it?

**Dale:** This is where you get me to say a number. [pause] Five grand. Less than five grand, it's somebody's nephew's app and it won't be around next year.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what annual price would it be a bargain?

**Dale:** Ten. Maybe twelve. That's what I spend on light towers in a quarter.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what annual price would it be getting expensive but still worth considering?

**Dale:** Twenty-five. That's where I have to go to Rich, and then it's a conversation about whether I believe in it.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what annual price would it be too expensive, no matter what?

**Dale:** From my side? Fifty. Anything over fifty I'm not asking for. If the owner mandates it, the owner can pay a hundred fifty or whatever they want. Then I'll use it, because I comply with owner mandates. I just won't like it.

**Interviewer (P6 probe):** You mentioned earlier that four days of float costs more than $150,000. How do you square that with a ceiling of $50,000?

**Interviewer (P7):** Which budget line would it come from, and what would it need to replace or prove to get funded? What evidence after a 12-week pilot would get you to sign?

**Dale:** Safety's budget, or the owner's. Not general conditions, that's my margin. If it's under twenty-five and I believed it, I'd find it, but I'd want safety to pay for it.

Replace? The observation cards. The two-a-week. If it's on top of the cards, it's dead. And whatever the safety guy does in Excel every Monday. It has to take something away.

**Interviewer (P7 probe):** What evidence after 12 weeks would get you to sign?

**Dale:** One thing. Show me one thing it caught that would've been a stop-work, and show me it got fixed before anybody had to stop. Like the panel. And show me my supers didn't spend one extra minute on it. Not a survey. I'll ask them myself at 6:30 and they'll tell me the truth.

And the owner didn't see it before I did. If I find out about something from the owner's queue, it's done.

**Interviewer (P7 probe):** What wouldn't count as evidence for you?

**Interviewer (P9):** Anything I should have asked but didn't? Who else should I talk to?

**Dale:** You should ask the guys, not the safety managers. Safety managers will buy anything. Talk to Hector, my electrical area super. He'll tell you the truth. Talk to the electrical foreman from the panel thing, if he'll talk to you. And talk to the owner's safety rep, she's the one that writes things up. That's it. I've got to go.
