## Section 1 — Warm-up

**Interviewer (W1):** Walk me through yesterday from the moment you got to the gate until you left. What took most of your attention?

**Park:** Yesterday was the last day of the Chuseok (추석) holiday, so it was not a normal day. Most of the yard was off. I went in at about 9:00 for the pre-restart inspection (연휴 후 작업재개 전 안전점검) because production restarts Monday morning. After a long holiday is one of the dangerous times. People come back tired, some come back late from their home country trips, equipment has been standing, gas lines have been closed and must be reopened. So I walked the erection area on the dock with two of my area safety officers and checked the gas manifold areas, the scaffolding (족장) in two blocks that are waiting for erection, and the temporary lighting inside the double-bottom sections. I left around 15:00.

If you want a normal day, the Wednesday before the holiday is better. I arrived 6:40. At 7:00 there is the morning HSE briefing with the division head's office. At 7:30 the subcontractors do their TBM (tool box meeting) in each bay, and my officers attend some of them. From 8:00 to about 9:30 I review what came in overnight: the portal, the KakaoTalk rooms, the gas-detector dashboard alarms from night shift. Then e-approval (결재) documents. Afternoon I usually walk one area. Then meetings. I left at 19:30.

**Interviewer (W1 probe):** Of all of that on the Wednesday, what took most of your attention?

**Park:** Honestly, the morning review. The KakaoTalk part. Many photos, and I have to figure out where each one is and whose it is. And the block delivery schedule. Before a holiday, production pushes to finish blocks for the dock, so there is overtime and more people inside blocks at the same time. That makes me nervous.

**Interviewer (W2):** Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day, other trades, other contractors or other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

**Park:** Many. Inside the company: the production teams (생산팀) for each bay, production planning (생산관리) who sets the block sequence, the facilities team for cranes and gas supply, and the CSO's office above me. Outside the company, but inside the yard: over 60 in-house subcontractors (사내협력사). Each one has a safety manager, some good, some just a title. Some of them also use tier-2 subcontractors. And sometimes the shipowner's supervisors, the class society, and of course MoEL and KOSHA when they visit.

How it happens: formal things go by e-approval or by meeting. The weekly subcontractor safety council (협력사 안전협의회) is where I meet the subcontractor safety managers together. The monthly joint OSH committee (산업안전보건위원회) with the union. Informal things, which is most of the daily volume, go by KakaoTalk and phone.

**Interviewer (W2 probe):** Can you give me an example of a coordination from last week that happened informally?

**Park:** A crane team needed to move a block over an area where one painting subcontractor had people working on the outside shell. The production foreman called my area officer, my officer called the painting subcontractor's safety manager, he sent a message in their KakaoTalk room, and the workers were moved. It took about forty minutes. It worked. But no record of it exists except the chat.

**Interviewer (W3):** When something on site looks "off" to you or your crew, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

**Park:** For my own staff, they take a photo, tell the responsible subcontractor's safety manager on the spot if they can find him, and post it in the KakaoTalk room for that area. If it is serious, they call me directly. Then it should go into the portal as an observation, but that part happens later, sometimes the next day.

Most recent example: last Tuesday, one of my officers found an oxygen hose and an LPG hose laid together across a walkway at the entrance of a block, no protection, and one had a damaged section taped over. He stopped the hot work in that block, called the subcontractor, they replaced the hose within about an hour. He posted the photo. In the portal it was entered Wednesday afternoon.

**Interviewer (W3 probe):** Why was it entered a day later?

**Park:** Because he was on the floor. Entering in the portal takes time: choose the area code, the subcontractor code, the category, write the description in Korean. On the phone it is not convenient. So they do it at the desk. This is normal.

**Interviewer (W4):** What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

**Park:** The company smart-safety portal from headquarters. It connects the IoT gas sensors, the access-control badges and the CCTV monitoring room. It is good for the sensors. For observations it is only as good as what people type in. KakaoTalk: fast, everybody has it, but photos come without location, without date information I can trust, and the rooms are a mess. Excel: my deputies keep the open-items list for the weekly council. Paper: the risk assessment (위험성평가) sheet for each TBM, the work permits (작업허가서) for hot work and confined space entry. Groupware e-approval for anything that goes up to the CSO. And the gas-detector dashboards.

What I like: e-approval, because it is clear who approved what and when. What I dislike: that the real information is in KakaoTalk and paper, and the official record is in the portal, and they do not match.

**Interviewer (W4 probe):** When they don't match, which one do you rely on?

**Park:** For knowing what is happening, KakaoTalk. For the official position of the company, the portal and e-approval. That gap is the thing I think about.

**Interviewer (W5):** How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

**Park:** Night shift has one duty safety officer. He sends me a short summary on KakaoTalk at around 6:00. If anything serious happens, he calls me, any hour. The CCTV monitoring room keeps a log. The gas-detector alarm history is in the dashboard. And the subcontractors report to their own management first, and then to us. That last part is where things are late.

**Interviewer (W5 probe):** How late?

**Park:** For injuries, usually the same day, because hospital visits are hard to hide. For near-misses, sometimes never. Sometimes I find out at the monthly quota submission, and the date written on it is not the real date.

## Section 2 — Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your crew or site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Park:** I will speak generally, because I do not want to point at a specific subcontractor. About two months ago, in a double-bottom block, the forced-ventilation duct was torn and the air inside was bad. Welders, mostly Thai and Vietnamese, were working there. They noticed. The fume was visible. They did not write anything. One of them told the 반장 (team leader) at lunch through another worker who speaks some Korean. The 반장 got a new duct section, and the work continued. We found out only because the gas detector in that block logged a low-oxygen warning that night, and my night officer asked about it the next morning. Then the story came out.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why do you think the workers didn't report it formally?

**Park:** Several reasons, and I am not sure which is the biggest. First, they cannot write Korean, and the portal is Korean. Second, their employer is the subcontractor, not us. They report to the 반장, and the 반장 decides. Third, if they report to the prime, the subcontractor might see it as making trouble, and their contract renewal depends on the subcontractor president. For the E-9 visa workers, the employer relationship is everything. I think they think, "if the problem is fixed, why make noise."

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** What would have happened to them if they had reported it directly to your team?

**Park:** To be honest, from my team, nothing bad. We would thank them. But I cannot promise what happens inside the subcontractor afterward. The 반장 might be scolded by his president because a prime officer came asking. Then the 반장 remembers who talked. I have heard of workers being moved to worse jobs. I cannot prove it.

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Park:** For Korean regular employees of the prime, usually nothing bad. They are protected by the union, and they are not afraid of subcontractors. For subcontractor workers, it depends on the company. I will give one good and one not good.

Good: last year an Uzbek fitter refused to enter a tank because the entry permit was not posted. He used the right to stop work (작업중지권). His subcontractor's safety manager supported him, and we used his case in the subcontractor council as a positive example. We gave a small award.

Not good: some time ago, a Nepali worker complained about scaffolding in another subcontractor's area, not his own. The other subcontractor's foreman came to his bay and shouted at him. After that his own 반장 told him, "Don't talk about other companies' things." I heard about it from our interpreter, not from any report.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Has it ever affected someone's assignments, contract, or reputation that you know of?

**Park:** Contract renewal, I cannot see. That is the subcontractor's decision. Assignments, yes, I believe so, from what the interpreters tell me. Reputation, yes. A worker who talks a lot becomes known. I would say the fear is real, but it is fear of the 반장 and the subcontractor president, not so much of us. But from the worker's side, maybe they do not see the difference between us and them.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard reported in the last month. What did the person who reported it see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Park:** The hose example from last Tuesday. My officer saw it closed in one hour, because he stayed there. The subcontractor workers, I think they saw the new hose. Nobody told them anything formally.

For items that come in through KakaoTalk from subcontractor safety managers, the "closure" is when the subcontractor posts a correction photo in the room. Then my deputy marks it closed in Excel. The worker who noticed it, if a worker noticed it, is not informed. We don't know who he is, in most cases.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What's the longest you've seen an item wait?

**Park:** An item that needs facilities work, like a broken lighting fixture on a high platform, or a missing handrail on a permanent structure, can wait three, four weeks, because it goes into the facilities work request queue. Those items are the ones I worry about. They stay open in the Excel, and each week at the council they are still there.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** How does that affect whether people report again?

**Park:** For my officers, they keep reporting because it is their job. For subcontractor safety managers, I think they learn which items are worth sending. For workers, I don't have data. I would guess if nothing visible happens, they stop.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report — cards, quotas, rewards, apps? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Park:** Many things. The main one is the near-miss (아차사고) quota. Each subcontractor must submit a number of near-miss reports every month, in proportion to headcount. It is part of their safety evaluation, which affects their evaluation score with us. So they submit. Most arrive in the last three days of the month. The same photo appears in different months. Some are written by the subcontractor office staff, not by anyone who saw anything. I will say this plainly since it is anonymous: the number is meaningless. But it is a KPI from headquarters, and it is also in my KPI.

Second, headquarters added a QR-code reporting page in the portal, a kind of "safety reporting box" (안전신문고). Korean only. In my division we get maybe two or three per month. Mostly from Korean regular employees, and half of them are about the parking or the shuttle bus.

Third, the stop-work right campaign, with small rewards, gift vouchers (상품권). A few cases per quarter.

Fourth, last year my division bought portable translation devices, about forty units, for the subcontractor safety managers to use at TBM. They were used for maybe two months. Now most are in drawers. The battery, the noise in the bay, and the translation of technical words was not good.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why do you think the translation devices faded?

**Park:** The noise first. In a bay with grinding and gouging, a device held in the hand does not hear well. Then the terms. A word like 족장 (scaffolding, shipyard slang) or 곤도라 (gondola), the device does not know. The workers laughed at some translations. Once people laugh at a tool, it is over. And no one was responsible for charging them.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** What did people say about the quota in the break rooms, as far as you know?

**Park:** Subcontractor safety managers call it "homework" (숙제). They say it to my face, jokingly. They are not wrong.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment someone notices a hazard mid-task: where are they, what's in their hands, what's around them, and how much time do they realistically have to tell someone?

**Park:** For a welder inside a block: he is in a double-bottom compartment, maybe 1.2 or 1.5 meters high, crouching or on his side, welding helmet down, leather gloves, torch in the hand, the cable behind him. It is hot, there is fume, the only light is the temporary lamp. The noise from the grinding next door is very loud. The nearest exit manhole might be twenty meters of crawling. He cannot tell anyone in that moment. He can tell someone when he comes out for a break, or at lunch.

For my officers on patrol it is different. Hands are free, phone is in the pocket.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What are the phone rules inside the blocks?

**Park:** For painting areas and confined spaces with explosive atmosphere, phones are not allowed inside at all, unless explosion-proof. In other areas, personal phones are allowed but officially no photos, because of shipowner confidentiality and technology protection. LNG carriers have containment technology that is sensitive. In practice, workers do take photos. We know. My own company phone has MDM, and the camera works only in some apps.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** What's the longest someone would spend telling someone before they would "just deal with it later"?

**Park:** For a migrant worker, the problem is not seconds. The problem is language. If he has to find a bilingual coworker, that can be an hour, or it is never. If he could speak in his own language into something, I think it could be quick. But I say this as a guess. I cannot speak for them.

**Interviewer (K1):** When a worker who doesn't speak Korean sees a hazard, how does that information reach the prime's safety team today? Walk me through a recent case.

**Park:** The path is long. Worker, then a bilingual coworker if there is one, then the 반장, then the subcontractor's safety manager, then KakaoTalk photo to my area officer, then my deputy puts it in Excel, then maybe it goes into the portal. At every step, it can stop.

A recent case, about three weeks ago: a Nepali worker noticed that the lifting lug on a small pre-outfitting unit had a crack at the weld. He showed it to his 반장 with his hands, by pointing, because the 반장 speaks no Nepali and he speaks very little Korean. The 반장 understood "problem with lug." He told the subcontractor safety manager, who sent a photo to my officer. The photo showed the lug, but not which unit, not which bay. It was sent two days later. By then the unit had already been lifted and moved. Nothing happened, fortunately, and we found the unit and the lug was re-inspected, and it was a real crack, a small one. But it was lifted with that crack.

**Interviewer (K1 probe):** How did you find out which unit it was?

**Park:** My officer went to the subcontractor office and asked. They called the 반장, the 반장 asked the worker, the worker described, and then we checked with production planning which units were lifted that day. Half a day of work for my officer.

**Interviewer (K1 probe):** How many languages are we talking about in your division?

**Park:** Among the subcontracted workers: Vietnamese is the largest group, then Thai, Uzbek, Nepali, Indonesian, some Sri Lankan, some Myanmar, some Chinese-Korean who speak Korean. We have a few contracted interpreters for the main languages. Not for all. And interpreters work day shift.

**Interviewer (K2):** Since the Serious Accidents Punishment Act, how has the way you record hazards and actions changed? What are you most careful about writing down?

**Park:** (pause) This is a sensitive question. I will answer carefully.

It has changed a lot. Before, safety was inspections and education hours. Now my job is to build the safety and health management system (안전보건관리체계) so that we can show the CEO and CSO fulfilled their duties. Everything must be documented. The risk assessment must show worker participation. The budget allocation for safety must be documented. The CSO's review of subcontractor safety must be documented. We do half-year reports to the CEO. So we record more than ever.

What am I most careful about: I am careful that a finding is never written alone. If we write "hazard identified," the action must be next to it: who, what measure, by when, and then the closure. A finding without an action is the worst kind of record. In an investigation, the question is always: "You knew. What did you do?" Our legal team trained us: write facts, not opinions, not guesses. Do not write "this may cause a fatality" in the minutes. Write the fact and the measure.

**Interviewer (K2 probe):** Can you give me an example of something you'd be careful about writing?

**Park:** For example, the facilities items I mentioned. If a missing handrail is recorded on the 3rd and closed on the 28th, and someone falls on the 20th, that record is, I will say it directly, exhibit A. So when my deputies record an item that we know will take weeks, they also record the interim measure: barricade, sign, restricted access. And they record that it was checked. Without the interim measure recorded, I don't want the item in the system.

Let me be clear: I do not mean hiding records. Destroying or hiding records is also a crime, and it is worse. I mean every record must have an owner and an action. That is the discipline.

**Interviewer (K2 probe):** How do records in KakaoTalk fit into that?

**Park:** That is the problem. In one case at another company, not ours, the investigators took the phones and went through the KakaoTalk rooms with digital forensics. So the KakaoTalk rooms are also records, but nobody manages them as records. There is a photo in a room from three weeks ago with no action recorded. That worries me more than the portal.

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost, or go to the wrong place?

**Park:** I described part of it. Where it slows down: first, between the worker and the 반장, because of language. Second, between the subcontractor and us, because the subcontractor wants to fix it quietly before telling us. Third, between my team and other departments of the prime, like facilities, because it becomes a work request in their system, and their system does not talk to ours.

Where it gets lost: KakaoTalk. A photo is posted at 14:00, then fifty messages come after it, and at 18:00 nobody remembers it.

Where it goes wrong: location. "Block 3" means nothing. Which ship, which block number, which deck, which compartment? And the block moves. A hazard in a block in the assembly shop on Monday is on the dock on Thursday, and the area officer is different.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Duplicates?

**Park:** Yes. My officer reports it, the subcontractor reports the same thing, the production foreman also mentions it in the production meeting. In Excel it becomes two lines, sometimes three, and at the council someone says "we fixed that already" and it turns out it is the same item.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Who decides who owns it?

**Park:** My area officer decides, based on who is working in the area. If it is between two subcontractors, he decides, and sometimes they argue. If it is prime facilities, it goes to facilities. I would say the officer decides correctly most of the time, but it depends on the officer. Some are experienced, some are two years in.

**Interviewer (C7):** How do you currently sort incoming observations to decide what's urgent and who owns it? How long does that take on a typical day?

**Park:** Every morning, my two deputies (차장) and I go through everything. From the night summary, the portal, and the KakaoTalk rooms. On a normal day, forty to sixty items in my division. At peak, more. It takes about an hour and a half for the three of us, so four or five person-hours per day, just sorting.

We decide urgent by experience. Anything with fall from height, confined space, gas, fire, heavy lifting is urgent. Those I want acted on the same day and, if serious, reported to the CSO by e-approval the same day.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What information do you need to decide, that you often don't have?

**Park:** Exact location. Whether work is currently happening there. Whether someone is exposed now or whether it is a condition for later. Which subcontractor, including tier-2. And whether it already has an interim measure. Most photos give me none of that.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What do you rebuild by hand?

**Park:** The weekly open-items list for the subcontractor council, by subcontractor. One deputy spends most of Friday afternoon on it. And the monthly figures for headquarters, which come from the portal but must be checked against Excel.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or instruction was passed along *automatically* or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Park:** Two examples. The gas sensors in the portal send alarms automatically to the area's safety officer and the production foreman. When a block moves from the assembly shop to the dock, the sensor must be re-tagged to the new area. Sometimes it is late. Then the alarm goes to the old area's officer, who says "that's not my area anymore," and does nothing, or forwards it. And there were many false alarms in the beginning, so people started ignoring the notifications. Alarm fatigue. That took about six months to fix, and I am not sure it is fully fixed.

Second: one subcontractor safety manager used a free translation app to translate a Korean safety instruction into Nepali and posted it in the room. Our interpreter told me later the meaning was wrong. "Do not enter without a gas test" became something like "gas test is not needed to enter." Nobody got hurt. But after that I told subcontractors not to use machine translation for safety instructions without checking.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore an automatically routed item?

**Park:** Trust: if a named person on my team has confirmed it before it goes to the subcontractor, and if the location is precise and cannot be changed afterward. Ignore: if it comes from a system, with no person, and if it has been wrong before. Once it is wrong two or three times, people ignore it. It is the same as the gas alarms.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** Who should be able to see who reported an item?

**Park:** (pause) My team should. Because we sometimes need to ask questions. "Which unit? Which lug?" If it is fully anonymous, I cannot follow up. But the subcontractor company, I think no, not by default. Because of what I said about the 반장 and the president. This is my personal view. The union might have another view, and the legal team might have another view.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment, or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Park:** This happens every day in a shipyard. The typical case: painting (도장) and hot work. Two weeks ago, a painting subcontractor had a touch-up crew inside a tank in a block, and an outfitting subcontractor was grinding and welding pipe supports on the deck above, directly over an opening. Solvent vapor and sparks. It was found by the fire watch of the outfitting crew, who smelled the paint. He stopped the work and called. My officer came, stopped the hot work for about two hours until the painting crew finished and the space was ventilated and gas-tested.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** How often does that happen in a week, and who owned the conflict?

**Park:** Conflicts like that, painting and hot work, crane lift over workers, two crews needing the same staging, I would say several times a week in my division. Maybe more that we don't see. Who owns it: production planning should, because they make the sequence. The permit system should catch it, because hot work needs a permit. In reality, at the moment of conflict, my officer owns it, because he is the one who stops it. And after, in the meeting, production planning says the sequence was fine and the subcontractors did not follow it.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** What did that two-hour stop cost?

**Park:** For that block, not much. Two hours for one crew. But if it happens on a block that is on the critical path for dock erection, two hours can become overtime for the weekend. I don't track that cost. Production knows it better.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation, or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Park:** Since the fatality in my division three years ago, we have had two partial work-stop orders (부분 작업중지명령) from MoEL. I will talk about the more recent one generally. It was last year. A section of scaffolding inside a block collapsed and two subcontractor workers were seriously injured, both needing months of treatment. Under the law, that is a serious accident, and MoEL issued a partial work-stop order on that bay. It lasted a little over a week until they accepted our improvement plan.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Was the hazard known beforehand? By whom?

**Park:** (pause) In the investigation, it came out that an Uzbek worker had told his 반장 about a loose clamp on that scaffolding a few days before. The 반장 said he told the scaffolding subcontractor. The scaffolding subcontractor said nobody told them. There was no record anywhere. No KakaoTalk, no portal, nothing. So in one way, the absence of a record protected nobody. It only showed that the system did not work. That was one of the reasons the headquarters audit later wrote the finding on "worker voice channels."

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** How was the cost to the schedule tracked, if at all?

**Park:** Production calculated the delay. The bay was stopped, the blocks in it were late to the dock, and they re-sequenced erection and recovered with weekend overtime. I saw a number in the report to management. I prefer not to say it. It was large, much larger than any safety budget item I approve. But in my experience, that number is used for one month after the accident, and then it is forgotten when the next year's budget is made.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, for example one crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Park:** Example: a painting subcontractor leaves solvent cans and rags at the entrance of a block, and the welding subcontractor's workers must pass through with hot-work equipment. The welding subcontractor's safety manager sends a photo to my officer. My officer tells the painting subcontractor's safety manager. The painting subcontractor cleans it, sends a photo back to the KakaoTalk room. My officer marks it closed.

Who proves it's closed: the photo. But I cannot be sure the photo is from today, or from that place. Some subcontractors send the same "after" photo more than once. My officers are supposed to verify on site for important items, and they do when they can.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help or don't?

**Park:** KakaoTalk helps the speed. It does not help the proof. The portal helps the proof, but nobody enters the closure in the portal on time, so the proof is late. The e-approval helps for the serious items, because the CSO sees them. For cross-subcontractor items, honestly, none of the tools are designed for it. The portal is organized by our internal departments.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** What would an owner, a regulator, or a lawyer ask to see?

**Park:** MoEL investigators and prosecutors ask: the risk assessment for that work, including worker participation; whether the hazard was identified; what measure was decided; who was responsible; when it was done; who verified; and whether the CSO and CEO were informed and allocated resources. They also ask for education records and the subcontractor evaluation records, because the prime has to evaluate subcontractors' safety capability. And, as I said, they may look at the phones. The shipowner's supervisors care less about this; they care about the delivery date and about incidents on their ship.

**Interviewer (C12):** How are tools for the site's safety or field coordination paid for today? Whose budget, per what unit — worker, seat, project, month — and who has to sign?

**Park:** Two levels. The smart-safety portal, sensors and CCTV platform are headquarters, paid from the group budget, contracted by headquarters purchasing. I don't pay for those, and I don't choose them.

My division has a safety budget line, which is required and documented under the SAPA management system. From that line I buy equipment: gas detectors, ventilation equipment, barriers, the translation devices, sometimes training content. I can approve up to about KRW 50 million per item. Above that, the CSO signs, and for anything new with software, 구매팀 (purchasing) must register the vendor and headquarters IT security must review it.

Units: equipment is per unit. Software for employees is per account, through IT. Subcontractor workers do not have company accounts, so per-account software does not reach them. That is a real limitation.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What happens with licensing or tools when subcontractor crews turn over?

**Park:** They turn over a lot. A subcontractor adds thirty workers for a peak and releases them two months later. Visa workers change employers sometimes. Any system that requires registering each person will be out of date in weeks. The access-control badges are the only thing that is always current, because nobody enters without a badge.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What was the last safety-related purchase, and how long did approval take?

**Park:** The translation devices: about KRW 18 million, existing equipment vendor, about six weeks. Before that, a VR safety-training program from a new vendor: about five months. Vendor registration took two months, with financial statements and a credit review. The IT security review took longer. IT required it to run on our internal servers, not on the vendor's cloud. For anything with worker data, IT will ask for ISMS-P certification or equivalent, and data stored in Korea. That is not negotiable at my level.

## Section 3 — Concept Presentation & Willingness-to-Pay

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'm going to read a short description, and then ask for your reactions. "Some teams are testing a tool where any worker can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items. The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Park:** Can I ask, is this a Korean company, or foreign?

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'd rather hear your reaction to the description itself first. If where the company is based would change your answer, please tell me how.

**Park:** It would change it, yes. For purchasing and IT security, a foreign company without a Korean entity and without in-country data storage is a much harder path. I'll answer about the description.

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Park:** If it worked as described, the biggest change would be the morning sorting and the KakaoTalk problem. If the report comes with a precise location, a translation, and a suggested subcontractor, and my officer confirms it, then my deputies do not spend Friday rebuilding the Excel. And the closure photo would be linked to the item. That part interests me, the closure being visible in the same place as the report.

Second, the migrant workers. If a Vietnamese or Nepali worker could say it in his language, it would be the first real worker-voice channel we have. That is the headquarters audit finding. I would be able to show the auditors something.

What it wouldn't change: the near-miss quota. That is a headquarters KPI and it will continue whatever tool I use. The production pressure. The relationship between the worker and his 반장 and his subcontractor president. If the worker is afraid of his president, a tool does not remove that. And it wouldn't change the facilities queue. If the fix takes three weeks, the tool will show three weeks.

**Interviewer (P1 probe):** What about the "what's the rule here?" part?

**Park:** Useful for my younger officers and for the subcontractor safety managers. Less for the workers, I think. But I have a concern. If it cites the law, the Occupational Safety and Health Rules (산업안전보건기준에 관한 규칙), fine. But our company standards are stricter than the law in many places. If it says "the law allows this," and our standard does not, a worker will follow the tool. And if the answer is wrong and someone gets hurt following it, who is responsible? It must cite our internal standards, and someone in my team must approve what it can say.

**Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you, or your crew, refuse to use it?

**Park:** Several things. I will list them.

One: the timestamp. Every report gets a time, and every open item is visible in one queue. That is good for management and very dangerous under SAPA if the volume is larger than my team can close. Today, there are 3,800 people in my division. If even a small part start reporting, the queue could be hundreds of items per week. If they sit open, each open item is a record that we knew. I would need to know that the tool records interim measures, not only open or closed, and that items can be classified properly. I cannot have a queue of red items with dates on them.

Two: the reviewer. "A human safety reviewer confirms." Who? My officers are already busy. Night shift has one person. Weekends at peak, reports would come at 2:00 in the morning in Uzbek. If nobody reviews until 8:00, then the record shows a six-hour gap. I would need headcount, or the subcontractor safety managers must be reviewers for their own areas, and that brings back the under-reporting.

Three: data and security. Voice recordings of workers are personal information under the Personal Information Protection Act (개인정보보호법). Where is the voice stored? Where is the translation done? If the translation or the "suggestion" is done by a server outside Korea, IT will reject it immediately. Also shipowner confidentiality. Photos inside the blocks of an LNG carrier cannot go to a foreign cloud.

Four: translation accuracy for safety terms. I told you about the translation devices and the Nepali instruction. If the system translates the worker's report wrongly, we may route it wrongly. If it translates our instruction wrongly back to the worker, it is dangerous.

Five: the relationship with the subcontractors. And there is a legal point. In Korean shipbuilding, the prime must be careful not to give direct work instructions to subcontractor workers, because of the illegal dispatch (불법파견) issue. Safety measures are permitted and required, but if a system sends tasks from the prime directly to subcontractor workers, the legal team will look at it carefully. The "responsible owner" must be the subcontractor company, through its safety manager, not the individual worker.

Six: phones. Personal phones and the photo restriction. Explosion-proof zones. Workers need to report from outside the block, at break, and then the location is not exact.

What would make workers refuse? If they think the subcontractor will see their name. And if they report and nothing comes back. For my officers, if it adds work instead of removing it. For the subcontractors, they will not refuse openly. They will simply make their workers not use it.

**Interviewer (P2 probe):** You said you'd need to know it records interim measures. What else would it have to show for that first concern to be acceptable?

**Park:** That every item has an owner within a short time, and that the action is recorded next to the finding, with photo, as I said about K2. And that the retention and deletion rules are set by our legal team, not by the vendor. And that my team, not the vendor, controls who sees what. If it only makes hazards more visible and not the actions, I would not introduce it. I would be creating evidence against my own CEO.

**Interviewer (P3):** Who on your site would use it most, and who would ignore or resist it?

**Park:** Use most: my area safety officers, if it saves them the portal entry. The younger migrant workers, Vietnamese especially, because they are used to using their phones for everything, Zalo, Facebook. Some bilingual 반장s, who today carry the translation burden and would be happy to be relieved of it.

Ignore or resist: the subcontractor presidents, strongly but quietly, because more reports mean a worse evaluation score for them. Some older Korean 반장s, who see it as the prime watching them. Production teams, if it creates stops. The union, I am not sure. They could support it as a worker channel, or they could say it is monitoring of workers and demand a say on the joint OSH committee. I think they would demand access to the data. And headquarters IT, until it passes security.

**Interviewer (P3 probe):** What would decide which way the union goes?

**Park:** Whether it is presented at the joint OSH committee before it is introduced, and whether the data is used for discipline. If one worker is disciplined because of something in the system, the union will oppose it and they will be right to. It must be for hazards, not for people.

**Interviewer (P5):** Imagine two ways to pay: (a) a project-level subscription at **$150,000 per project-year**, which is roughly **KRW 200 million**, covering everyone on site, all employers included; or (b) traditional per-seat licensing at about **$10 per user per month**. How would each land with you, and why?

**Park:** First, "project" is not how we think. We have hulls, and we have divisions. My division works on several hulls at the same time, with the same workers. So for me, one "project" would be my division for one year.

KRW 200 million per year: that is four times my approval authority. It would go to the CSO, and then to 구매팀 and headquarters IT. I would have to prepare a business case. For a software tool from a new vendor, in my division alone, KRW 200 million is a large number. I don't think I could get that approved for a first year. Perhaps after a pilot, if the result was clear, and if headquarters saw it as something for all divisions.

Of course, if you compare it with one work-stop order, it is small. But I will be honest: nobody approves budget by comparing with an accident that has not happened. Budget is compared with last year's budget.

Per-seat, $10 per user per month: for 3,800 workers, that is more than KRW 600 million per year. Impossible. And the number changes every month. And purchasing would ask why the prime is paying licenses for each subcontractor worker, which brings back the legal question. Per-seat does not fit a shipyard. The all-employers approach fits better. The price is the difficulty.

**Interviewer (P5 probe):** Of the two structures, setting price aside, which would purchasing and IT find easier to process?

**Park:** A fixed annual contract per division, without individual accounts. Purchasing likes a fixed amount. IT likes fewer accounts, but they will ask how workers are identified, if not by accounts. Probably through the badge system. That would be the question.

**Interviewer (P5b):** Another version being considered is **$24,000 per project-year**, roughly **KRW 33 million**, sized for a ~200-person resident core team. How does that compare?

**Park:** KRW 33 million is within my authority. I could approve it from my division safety budget, after the vendor registration and IT review, which I cannot avoid in either case. So from an approval view, much easier.

But I need to understand the "200-person core team." Who are the 200? If it means my officers, my deputies, the subcontractor safety managers and the 반장s as reviewers and owners, and all workers can still report without a license, then it fits well. If it means only 200 people can report, then it does not solve my problem at all. The people I need to hear from are the 2,600-plus subcontracted workers, not my own staff. My staff already report.

**Interviewer (P5b probe):** How would your reaction differ between those two readings?

**Park:** First reading: I would be interested in a pilot at this price. Second reading: I would not buy it, even at this price, because the audit finding is about worker voice. A tool for 200 managers is another portal.

**Interviewer (P6):** Thinking of your division for one year as the unit, at what annual price would this be **so cheap** that you'd doubt it?

**Park:** Below about KRW 10 million per year. About $7,000. At that price, I would doubt that the vendor can pay for ISMS-P, Korean data storage, support in the night, and translation for eight or nine languages. I would worry the company disappears in two years and my records with it. Purchasing would also have a concern about the vendor's financial stability.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what price would it be **a bargain**, a good deal for what you get?

**Park:** Around KRW 30 million. About $22,000. At that level I can approve it myself, and if it works for the workers and for closure, it is good value.

**Interviewer (P6):** At what price would it be **getting expensive, but still worth considering**?

**Park:** About KRW 80 million. Roughly $58,000. That goes to the CSO. I would need pilot data to bring to him, and he would ask me what it replaces.

**Interviewer (P6):** And at what price would it be **too expensive, no matter what**?

**Park:** For my division alone, above KRW 150 million per year. About $110,000. At that level, I would not bring it forward. If headquarters wanted to buy it for the whole group, several divisions and several yards, that is a different calculation, and it is not my decision. I would only be asked for my opinion.

**Interviewer (P6 probe):** Is there anything in how your budget cycle works that affects these numbers?

**Park:** Timing. The budget for next year is being planned now, this autumn. Anything that is not in the plan by around November is difficult until the next year, unless it is under my authority. So a small first-year amount, under KRW 50 million, is much more realistic. And the first year must be positioned as part of the SAPA management system budget, which is protected. Protected, but also reviewed.

**Interviewer (P7):** Which budget line would it come from, and what would it need to replace or prove to get funded? What evidence after a 12-week pilot would get you to sign?

**Park:** The line: the division safety and health budget under the SAPA management system. Possibly some part from the subcontractor co-prosperity (상생협력) safety support program, which the group uses to support subcontractor safety. That would need another department's agreement.

What it replaces: realistically, part of the KakaoTalk process and the Excel, and some of the interpreter burden. I cannot say it replaces the portal, because the portal is headquarters. If it does not connect to the portal, at least by sending closed records into it, headquarters will ask why I have a separate system. That is a serious issue for me.

Evidence after 12 weeks. Let me be specific.

First: reports from subcontracted migrant workers, in their own languages, across several nationalities, not only Vietnamese, and not concentrated in the first two weeks. If week 10 and week 12 still have reports, that means something.

Second: closure. Time to owner assignment, time to interim measure, and time to closure with photo, by subcontractor. And the number of items open more than 7 days without an interim measure. That number must be close to zero, or I cannot continue.

Third: accuracy. My officers should check how often the suggested owner and category were correct. And our interpreters should check a sample of translations. If the translations fail on shipyard terms, it is over.

Fourth: my team's time. Did the morning sorting go down, or up?

Fifth: nothing from the security side. No incident, IT review passed, data in Korea, and the legal team satisfied with retention and access.

Sixth: at least one case where a worker's report found something serious before it became an accident. I say this carefully, because I cannot plan for it. But honestly, that is what convinces a CSO.

And I would want the union and the subcontractor council to have been informed before the pilot, so that it is not attacked later.

**Interviewer (P7 probe):** If the pilot showed strong reporting but slow closure, what would you do?

**Park:** Stop it, or reduce the scope, and fix the closure side first. Strong reporting with slow closure is the worst result for me. It is exactly the record I told you about.

**Interviewer (P7 probe):** Who else would have to agree before you signed, even at the lower price?

**Park:** Even under my authority, I would inform the CSO. 구매팀 for registration. Headquarters IT security. The legal team for personal information and retention. And practically, I would discuss it at the joint OSH committee. I would not introduce a worker channel without the union knowing. That would create a bigger problem than the one it solves.

**Interviewer (P9):** Anything I should have asked but didn't? Who else should I talk to?

**Park:** You did not ask about what happens to the data after the pilot. If we stop, where do the records go? Who deletes them? That question will come from legal first.

Who to talk to: subcontractor safety managers, several of them, from a good subcontractor and a weak one. The bilingual 반장s and our contracted interpreters, because they are the real channel today. Someone from headquarters IT security and from 구매팀, to understand the process, although they will not talk freely to an outside researcher. The union branch's safety officer. And of course the migrant workers themselves.

One request. If you speak with the migrant workers, please do not arrange it through the prime or through their subcontractor. If we arrange it, they will tell you what they think we want to hear. That is the same problem as the one we have talked about for an hour.

**Interviewer (Close):** Thank you for your time today.

**Park:** Thank you. Please remember what I said about the company name.
