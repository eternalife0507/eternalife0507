## Section 1 — Warm-up

**Interviewer (W1):** Walk me through yesterday from the moment you got to the gate until you left. What took most of your attention?

**Bob:** Yesterday was Saturday, so I never got to the gate. I spent two hours on a Teams call from my kitchen arguing about the Building 2 integrated systems test. I'll give you Thursday instead, since that was a site day.

I'm in the owner's trailer by 6:15. The first hour is email. Two things came in overnight: a utility letter about the second transformer at the substation, and a flash report from one of the GCs about a first-aid case, a hand laceration. At seven I have the owner-side stand-up with my building leads and my scheduler. It's twenty minutes, and it's basically "where are we against energization, and where are we against MW online." At nine I did my weekly walk, Building 3 and the MEP yard this time. That's about two hours with the GC's PD, their safety director and my owner HSE lead. The afternoon was a commercial meeting on a change order. The Building 4 GC wants eleven weeks of extended general conditions because our owner-furnished switchgear showed up late, and that's a fight. Then a P6 summary review with the scheduler. I left around 6:30.

What took most of my attention was the switchgear claim, honestly. That's real money, and whatever we do there sets precedent for the other three GCs.

**Interviewer (W1 probe):** On the walk itself, what were you paying attention to?

**Bob:** Progress first, if I'm honest. Is the hall where the three-week lookahead says it is? Then housekeeping, because housekeeping tells you about the superintendent. Then anything that looks like it could kill somebody: open edges, suspended loads, anything near energized gear. I'm not doing an inspection. I'm checking whether the contractor is in control.

**Interviewer (W2):** Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day, whether that's other trades, other contractors or other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

**Bob:** Four GCs, more or less one per building, plus the site/civil contractor and the substation contractor. Under them there are dozens of subs. I don't manage the subs, and I shouldn't. On my side there's the owner-furnished equipment team, because we buy the generators, switchgear, UPS and transformers. Then the commissioning agent, the design-of-record firm, the utility, operations (they take the halls at turnover) and my SVP.

As for how it happens, it's meetings and paper. There's a weekly PD meeting with all four GCs on Tuesday mornings, and a weekly site logistics meeting for cranes, laydown, gates and road closures. Day to day it's Teams chats. When it's about money or time it goes in a formal letter, because I want anything that could turn into a claim on letterhead. My trust with the GC PDs is moderate. They're good firms, and they also have their own P&Ls.

**Interviewer (W3):** When something on site looks "off" to you, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

**Bob:** Thursday, actually. In the MEP yard behind Building 3, a telehandler was setting pipe spools, and a cable-pulling crew from a different sub was working the duct bank right at the edge of where the telehandler was swinging. There was no barricade between them and no spotter that I could see. I didn't stop it myself. I turned to the GC's safety director, who was walking with me, and asked, "Is that in your plan?" He got on the radio, and within a couple of minutes somebody had put up a hard barricade and moved the cable crew back about twenty feet.

**Interviewer (W3 probe):** What happened after that?

**Bob:** Friday morning I got an email from the GC safety director. It was one paragraph and a photo: "Observation logged, corrected on the spot, discussed at Friday toolbox talk." That's it. I have no idea whether it's in their system or whether it was the third time that week. I'd have to ask.

**Interviewer (W4):** What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

**Bob:** The owner PMIS is our system of record for cost, changes, RFIs and submittals. We have PowerBI dashboards on top of it. I don't live in P6. My scheduler does, and I get the summary and the critical path. Then Outlook and Teams, and that's it. I don't carry a tablet on site or use apps on my phone out there. It's calls and email.

What I like is PowerBI, when the data's good, because I can see a trend in thirty seconds. The PMIS is fine as a record. What I dislike is that safety data isn't in the PMIS in any meaningful way. Each GC runs its own safety system, and what I get is whatever they upload monthly. Every one of them defines "observation" differently. One GC counts a positive observation, somebody wearing their glasses, the same as a hazard. So my dashboard says 6,000 observations and I don't know what that means.

**Interviewer (W4 probe):** What about permits?

**Bob:** Permits belong to the GCs: hot work, confined space, excavation, lifts. The exception is energized work and anything touching owner-furnished gear after it's been energized. Our commissioning agent controls those permits, and eventually our operations team does. That seam is where I lose sleep. I'll probably come back to it.

**Interviewer (W5):** How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

**Bob:** Four ways. The first is the flash report. Our contracts require the GCs to send a flash within two hours of any recordable, any SIF-potential event, or any dropped object over a set height. The second is my owner HSE lead. I have one lead and two field reps for a site that peaks around 4,500 workers, so she hears things. The third is the monthly dashboards and the monthly owner-contractor HSE leadership meeting, which I chair. The fourth is rumor. My building leads hear things in the trailers.

The honest answer is that I find out whatever the contractors decide is worth telling me, plus whatever my three HSE people happen to catch. That's not a knock on the GCs. It's structural.

## Section 2 — Core Assessment

**Interviewer (C1):** Tell me about the last time someone on your site noticed something unsafe and *didn't* formally write it up. What was going on?

**Bob:** I'll tell you up front that I'm the wrong person for the crew-level version of that. I don't know what doesn't get written up on my site, because by definition it never reaches me. You'd have to ask foremen.

What I can tell you about is our sister campus. Eighteen months ago we had a fatality there. An electrician on a subcontractor's crew contacted an energized bus in a switchgear lineup during a backfeed. I sat on the serious-incident review. The interviews showed that at least two people had raised a concern about the energization sequence. The lineup was partially energized, and the labeling hadn't caught up. One of them raised it at a toolbox talk. The other mentioned it to a commissioning tech from another company. Neither one was written up anywhere. It was just talk.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** Why not, as far as the review could tell?

**Bob:** The review called it "normalized schedule pressure." In plain English, energization was a milestone, everybody knew it was a milestone, and nobody wanted to be the guy who held it up over equipment that wasn't his company's. The electrician's employer didn't own that switchgear. We did, because it was owner-furnished, and the commissioning agent was running the sequence. So whose problem was it? That was the question in the room, and nobody answered it until somebody died.

**Interviewer (C1 probe):** What would have happened to them if they had written it up?

**Bob:** Officially, nothing. Every one of our contracts has stop-work authority, no-retaliation language, all of it. Realistically, I don't know. I spent twenty years on the EPC side, and I know what "officially nothing" sometimes means.

**Interviewer (C2):** When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to *the person who raised it*? Can you share an example, good or bad?

**Bob:** Again, nobody tells the owner that. If a guy gets moved to nights because he made his foreman look bad, it doesn't show up in a flash report.

From my EPC days, yes. On a refinery job about fifteen years back, a pipefitter foreman flagged a scaffold another contractor had built. The scaffold was bad, and he was right. Two weeks later his crew got moved to the worst work on the unit. Nobody said why, and everybody knew why.

On my campus today, here's the one I know about. A sub's safety coordinator on Building 1 had a concern about a lift plan for another GC's crane that was working near the boundary. He went around his own GC, straight to my HSE lead. The concern was valid. His GC was furious that he'd gone to the owner. As far as I know he's still on the job, but I'd bet he doesn't do that again.

**Interviewer (C2 probe):** Has it affected anyone's assignments or layoffs, that you know of?

**Bob:** Not that I know of. But this is Texas, non-union and at-will. When a building winds down and a sub cuts forty people, nobody writes down why it was those forty. So I can't tell you it hasn't happened either.

**Interviewer (C3):** Think of a hazard you reported in the last month. What did you see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

**Bob:** The one on Thursday got an email and a photo the next morning. About three weeks before that, there was blocked egress in a Building 2 data hall, with cable reels staged in front of an exit door. I had a photo back in about two hours showing it cleared.

You need to understand why, though. I sign the pay applications, so when I point at something it gets fixed fast. My reports aren't a representative sample of anything.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** What's the longest you've waited?

**Bob:** That was a cross-contractor item in the Building 3 MEP yard. The mechanical sub was working on a mezzanine above the electrical sub's work area. Part of the mezzanine edge had no toe boards, and material was stacked near that edge. My HSE field rep flagged it. The electrical side said it was the mechanical side's area. The mechanical side said the mezzanine had been turned over to the other GC's scope. It bounced around for about three weeks and came up in two weekly logistics meetings before it got closed. I'll come back to that one, because we later had a dropped object off that mezzanine.

**Interviewer (C3 probe):** How did that affect whether you, or your rep, would raise something like that again?

**Bob:** She'd raise it again, because that's her job. What changed is how she raises it. Now she goes straight to both GC PDs and copies me, because she learned that's the only way it moves. So I'm the routing system, and that doesn't scale.

**Interviewer (C4):** What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report, like cards, quotas, rewards or apps? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

**Bob:** We've tried everything. On the EPC side we had observation cards, quotas per supervisor and raffles. We once raffled off a truck on a chemical plant job. Participation spiked, and then we got four hundred cards that said "housekeeping good."

On the owner side, our portfolio standard requires each GC to report leading indicators, including observations per hundred workers. Within about three months it did exactly what you'd expect. The numbers went up, with a spike every month-end. One GC submitted over four thousand observations in a single month, something like ninety-seven percent of them positive. I had my HSE lead pull a sample, and a lot of them were the same observation reworded slightly.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** Why did it fade, or stick?

**Bob:** It didn't fade, and that's the problem. It stuck as a number. The number is still on my dashboard every month. It just stopped meaning anything. When you pay for volume, you get volume. What I never got was anything telling me whether the things that matter got closed.

**Interviewer (C4 probe):** What did people say about it, the GCs or anyone else?

**Bob:** A GC safety director told me over a beer, "You want cards, we'll give you cards." That about sums it up.

**Interviewer (C5):** Describe the moment someone notices a hazard mid-task. Where are they, what's in their hands, what's around them, and how much time do they realistically have to tell someone?

**Bob:** On a walk, I have all the time in the world. I'm in PPE, I'm escorted, my hands are empty, and the person I'd tell is standing right next to me. So I'm not your case.

For crews, I'd be guessing. I can describe the environment, though. The yards are loud with generators on test and load banks running in Building 2. It's a hundred and five degrees in August. A lot of the workforce is Spanish-first, with some Vietnamese and a few other languages, and we don't track that well. People have gloves on, and they're up on lifts and ladders.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** Are there phone rules?

**Bob:** Yes, and this matters. During construction, phones are allowed with the usual rules: not on a lift, not while operating equipment. Once a hall moves toward turnover and the security perimeter goes up, it becomes a controlled space. After a certain point, personal cameras aren't allowed in the white space. As we get close to live customer load, it's badge-controlled and phones get restricted further. Building 1 is energized and partly live. So in some of my most dangerous spaces, the live electrical ones, photos are exactly what my security people don't want.

**Interviewer (C5 probe):** How long do you think someone would spend before they'd "just deal with it later"?

**Bob:** I don't know. Not long, I'd guess. But that's a guess, and I'd rather not hand you a guess dressed up as data.

**Interviewer (C6):** When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

**Bob:** Within a single GC I assume it works reasonably well. Each GC has its own system. One uses Procore's observation module, one uses an inspection app, one has an in-house system, and one, I'm told, is mostly paper cards that an admin keys in weekly. A foreman writes it up, it goes to the GC safety team, they assign it to a sub, the sub fixes it, and it gets closed. I don't see any of that.

From where I sit, it breaks in three places. The first is between GCs, like the mezzanine. The second is owner-furnished equipment: the switchgear vendor's field techs, the generator vendor's startup crew. They don't work for any GC. They work for us, sort of, through the purchase order, so a hazard involving them has no obvious home. The third is commissioning. Once the CxA and operations take control of a space, construction reporting and operations reporting are two different worlds.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** Who decides who owns an item when it isn't obvious?

**Bob:** By default it's the GC nearest to it. If they push back, it goes to the weekly logistics meeting. If that doesn't work, it goes to my HSE lead and then to me. So a hazard that isn't obvious can sit for a week before anyone with authority even sees it.

**Interviewer (C6 probe):** What about duplicates, or missing information like location or photos?

**Bob:** I don't know about duplicates at the source. At the dashboard level I can't dedupe anything, because I get counts, not items. I've asked for item-level data, and what I get is PDFs.

**Interviewer (C7):** How do you currently sort incoming observations to decide what's urgent and who owns it? How long does that take on a typical day?

**Bob:** I don't sort them, and my HSE lead doesn't really either. The GCs' safety managers do that for their own contracts. My lead reviews the SIF-potential items and the flash reports and decides what comes to me. Under our standard, I hear about anything SIF-potential within 24 hours.

As for how long it takes her, I'd guess a couple of hours a day just reading GC reports and chasing people. At month-end she loses two or three days reconciling the four dashboards into one for the leadership meeting. She could give you a better number than I can.

**Interviewer (C7 probe):** What gets classified wrong most often?

**Bob:** Severity, and SIF potential specifically. One GC flags everything near energized gear as SIF-potential, and another almost never uses the flag. So when I look across the campus, Building 4 looks like the most dangerous building on site. I don't believe that. I think it has the most honest GC.

**Interviewer (C8):** Tell me about a time a report or instruction was passed along *automatically*, or by someone who didn't know the area well. How did people react?

**Bob:** Early on, one of the GCs set up their safety system to auto-email me every observation logged on their building. That was hundreds a week. Within about four days I had an Outlook rule dumping them into a folder, and I've never opened that folder.

The other one was after the sister-campus fatality. The portfolio funded camera-based monitoring of the crane zones at a couple of campuses, mine included. The system generated alerts for things like a person in the swing radius or a missing hard hat, and they went to the GC safety teams automatically. Within about a month the GCs were telling me most of the alerts were false positives, or just the flagger standing where he's supposed to stand. They stopped looking. I can't prove whether they stopped looking at the real ones too.

**Interviewer (C8 probe):** What would make you trust or ignore an automatically routed item?

**Bob:** Volume and hit rate. If the first ten things it sends me are real, I'll read the eleventh. If three of the first ten are junk, I'm done, and so is everybody else. I also want to know which human put their name on it. An alert with nobody's name on it is noise. When my HSE lead or a GC safety manager says "I looked at this, it's real, and it's yours," people move.

**Interviewer (C9):** Describe the last time two crews or contractors needed the same space, equipment or permit window at the same time. How was it discovered and resolved?

**Bob:** This is data centers, so it happens every week. The most recent real one was about three weeks ago. Building 2 was running load bank testing for level 4 IST. That means cable runs, load banks on the pad, energized equipment and a controlled perimeter. The same morning, the Building 3 GC had a crane pick scheduled for rooftop air-cooled chillers. The crane setup and counterweight swing came across the shared access road, which the Building 2 load bank cabling was crossing under a temporary cable protector.

It was discovered at about 6:40 a.m., when the crane crew showed up to set outriggers and the CxA's people told them to leave. The two sides didn't have it on the logistics board the same way. We resolved it by standing down the pick. The crane sat, and it picked the next morning.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** What did it cost in time?

**Bob:** The pick slipped a day. The GC will put the crane and rigging crew standby in as a cost, and I'd guess thirty to forty thousand once the crew is included. By luck it wasn't on the critical path that time. If the IST had been the thing that stopped, it would have been different, because IST is on the path to MW online.

**Interviewer (C9 probe):** Who "owned" the conflict?

**Bob:** *[pause]* Site logistics is an owner function on this campus, so we did. That's the uncomfortable answer. We own the logistics board, and we didn't catch it.

**Interviewer (C10):** Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or safety stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

**Bob:** The most recent one on my campus was last month, at the mezzanine I mentioned. A 24-inch pipe wrench fell about thirty-five feet off that mezzanine and landed maybe six feet from an electrician. Nobody was hurt. The GC stood down the Building 3 MEP yard for two days: safety review, retraining, toe boards installed, the whole thing.

The one that actually matters to me is the sister campus. After the fatality there was a full three-week campus stand-down. After recovery and re-sequencing, capacity delivery on the first hall slipped a little over five weeks net.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** Was the hazard known beforehand? By whom?

**Bob:** In both cases, yes. The mezzanine had an open item, the one my rep raised that bounced for three weeks, and it was marked closed. The toe boards went in on the section she flagged, not along the whole edge. On the sister campus, as I said, people knew. So either the hazard was known and closed on paper, or it was known and never made it onto paper at all.

**Interviewer (C10 probe):** How is that cost tracked, if at all? LDs?

**Bob:** Badly. There's no stoppage-hours line anywhere in my PMIS. What I have is the GC's daily reports, which show the stand-down; P6 variance, which shows the slip if it's on the path; and eventually a change order request or a claim, if the GC thinks it wasn't their fault. The GC is eating the two-day mezzanine stand-down because it was their sub. The crane pick is going to turn into a claim against us, because logistics is ours.

On LDs: on paper, the GCs owe us delay damages. In practice we almost never assess them. Assess LDs and you get a counterclaim and a year in a conference room. The real cost is on the business side, because a hall that isn't online isn't earning. I won't give you our revenue number per MW-day. It's big enough that the SVP knows the sister campus date slip by heart.

**Interviewer (C11):** When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, where one crew spots it and another company created it, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

**Bob:** Right now, whoever sends the last email. The contractor who created it says "fixed," attaches a photo if you're lucky, and the item goes to closed in their system. The contractor who spotted it may never hear back. Nobody independent verifies it unless my people go and look. The mezzanine is the perfect example. It was closed, and it wasn't fixed.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** Which tools help or don't?

**Bob:** None of them help across that line. Each GC's system stops at its contract. The PMIS doesn't do safety. The cameras don't know who owns anything. Our prequalification system tells me a sub's EMR and whether their insurance is current. It tells me nothing about an open hazard on a Tuesday.

**Interviewer (C11 probe):** What would an owner or a lawyer ask to see?

**Bob:** After the sister campus, our counsel asked for exactly this: who knew, when they knew, who it was assigned to, what was done and who verified it, all with timestamps. It took weeks to reconstruct from emails, toolbox sign-in sheets and three different systems, and some of it didn't exist.

There's another side to it that you should hear. Our legal department is also careful about owners holding detailed hazard data. Under OSHA's multi-employer policy, the more the owner directs and tracks the work, the closer we get to being a controlling employer. So counsel wants the record to exist when something happens, and they also want us not to look like we're running the contractors' safety programs. Both at once. I'm not saying that's coherent. I'm saying that's the conversation.

**Interviewer (C12):** How are tools for the site's safety or field coordination paid for today? Whose budget, per what unit (worker, seat, project, month), and who has to sign?

**Bob:** There are two buckets. The GCs' safety tools belong to the GCs. They pay for them and recover the cost through general conditions, which on a GMP means I'm paying for it anyway. I just don't see a line item. Whether that's per seat, per project or enterprise depends on the deal they have with their vendor.

Owner-side tools like the PMIS and PowerBI are enterprise licenses owned by our delivery org and funded centrally. I don't buy those. For project-specific tools or services, I can approve up to about five hundred thousand out of the project budget without going to the SVP. But anything that's software, even under five hundred, has to go through IT security review and procurement. That takes three to six months for a new vendor, and longer if it touches any of our networks.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What was the last safety-related purchase, and how long did approval take?

**Bob:** The crane-zone camera monitoring, about a hundred and eighty thousand for my campus for one year. It was approved in about six weeks, which is fast, and the only reason it moved that fast was that it came right after the sister campus. Money moves after a fatality. Now, eighteen months later, it's up for renewal and I'm not sure I'll renew it. I have a lot of video, and I can't tell you it prevented anything.

We also have quarterly third-party safety audits, where an independent firm walks every GC. That's a service contract out of the owner HSE budget, and that one I'd keep.

**Interviewer (C12 probe):** What happens when crews turn over weekly? Does that affect how things are bought?

**Bob:** At peak we badge something like two to four hundred new workers a week, and about the same number leave. The badging office can barely keep an accurate headcount. Anything priced per person on this site would be a reconciliation nightmare and a monthly argument with the GCs. So per-seat anything is a non-starter site-wide. It works for my owner team of forty people.

## Section 3 — Concept Presentation & Willingness-to-Pay

**Interviewer (Concept):** I'd like to shift now. I'll describe a concept and then ask for your reaction. I'm going to read it exactly as written.

> "Some teams are testing a tool where any worker can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items. The reporter is notified when it's verified closed. It can also answer 'what's the rule here?' with the source cited. It's priced per project, not per user."

**Interviewer (P1):** In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

**Bob:** My week? Probably not much, directly. I don't report hazards, and I'm not going to be the reviewer.

The line I heard was "the owner sees one queue of open items." If that were true across all four GCs, the owner-furnished vendors and the CxA, then my HSE lead's month-end reconciliation goes away. And a mezzanine-type item doesn't bounce for three weeks, because everybody can see it. That would change her week, and it would change what I bring into the monthly leadership meeting.

What it wouldn't change is that contractors still own execution. The GC still has to fix it. It doesn't change my commercial exposure, and it doesn't change my logistics board unless it's connected to it. And I'll tell you, every vendor who's walked into my trailer has promised "one pane of glass." Is there any data showing this has worked on a site like mine?

**Interviewer (P1 probe):** I'm not in a position to speak to results today. I'm here to understand what you'd need to see. When you say "if that were true," what would it take for it to be true?

**Bob:** All four GCs using it. Using it, not "integrating" with it. And that doesn't happen because I ask nicely. It happens because it's in the contract, and those contracts are already signed. So on this campus I'd be asking GCs to change process mid-job, and they'll say yes and then price it.

**Interviewer (P2):** What worries you about it? What would make you, or others on your site, refuse to use it?

**Bob:** I'll go in order.

First, GC adoption. Their corporate safety departments mandate their own systems. A fifth system means double entry, and double entry dies in about six weeks.

Second, legal, meaning the controlling-employer question. If I'm the one holding "one queue of open items," counsel will ask whether I've just taken on the contractors' safety obligations. They might be fine with it, or they might not.

Third, security. That's photos and voice recordings from inside data halls, stored by a vendor. Before anything else, my security people will ask where the data sits and who can see it. And as I said, cameras are the problem in the controlled spaces.

Fourth, "the system suggests a severity." Suppose it calls a SIF-potential item low, the human reviewer is buried and clicks confirm, and then something happens. That's the headline I'm paid to avoid.

Fifth, "a human safety reviewer." Whose human? If it's my HSE team, that's headcount I don't have. If it's the GCs', then four GCs have to agree on how to review.

As for refusing, the GCs would refuse if it turned into a scorecard on them that I used in commercial conversations. And they'd be right to worry, because I would use it that way.

**Interviewer (P2 probe):** On the GC side, what would they need to see to not treat it as a fifth system?

**Bob:** It would have to replace something of theirs, or feed their system so they don't have to re-key anything. And their safety directors would need to believe it isn't going to be used to hammer them in the monthly meeting. I don't know how you solve that one.

**Interviewer (P3):** Who on your site would use it most, and who would ignore or resist it?

**Bob:** My HSE lead would use it most, right away. The smaller GC, the one with the paper cards, probably would too, because it's better than what they have. Maybe the CxA, since they live at the seam. For the crews you'd have to ask them. The language piece might matter, since we have a lot of Spanish-first workers, but I'm guessing.

The largest GC would resist. They're a national firm with an enterprise safety platform and a corporate mandate, and their safety director will politely tell me their system already does this. Superintendents would resist if it creates items faster than they can close them, because now there's a visible list with their name on it. So would operations once they take a hall. They have their own work order and incident systems, and they won't want construction's tool in their space.

**Interviewer (P5):** Imagine two ways to pay: (a) a project-level subscription at $24,000 per project-year covering everyone on site, all employers included, or (b) traditional per-seat licensing at about $10 per user per month. The $24,000 version is sized for a resident core team of about 200 people. How would each land with you, and why?

**Bob:** Two hundred people, and it covers everyone on site? Which is it? I've got four thousand-plus at peak.

**Interviewer (P5 clarification):** The description I have says it's sized for a core team of roughly 200 residents. I don't have any more detail on how it handles people beyond that.

**Bob:** Okay. Let's take (a). Twenty-four thousand is a rounding error on this campus. It's honestly below the level where I think about it. I could approve that on a Friday afternoon.
