Before you go to siteHow to use this guide

This is version 2 of the SAiFETY Talk interview instrument, rewritten after a 10-persona synthetic study. Each question shows two notes. Synthetic study suggests is what the simulation produced, and it is a hypothesis only. Validate with real humans is what this interview has to establish. Score each key assumption from what the respondent volunteers before Section 3, using the grid at the end.

Who to recruit (Step 21)

  • Forepersons and 반장, 8–10
  • Crew: apprentices, travelers, migrant subcontracted workers, 8–10
  • GC safety coordinators who triage reports, 5–6
  • Union stewards or business agents, 3–4
  • Night-shift foremen, 3
  • Site safety managers, EHS directors, owner PDs, 6–8
  • Korea: HSE team leads and prime purchasing or IT security, 3–4

Consent and protection

  • Recruit frontline and migrant workers through the union hall or community groups, never through their employer.
  • Don't record names for frontline respondents. Use a code such as F-03.
  • Say plainly: “Nothing you say goes to your employer. You can skip any question.”
  • Use an interpreter who has no tie to the prime or the subcontractor.
  • Meet off site or at break, away from supervisors.

Interviewer rules

  • No product, AI, app or BuiltNOVA mention before Section 3.
  • Ask about the last specific time, not “would you…”.
  • Don't say “problem”, “pain” or “solution” first.
  • Follow up with: What happened next? Why? Can you show me?
  • Don't agree, praise or explain. Accept silence.

Bring with you

  • A printed mock routed item for C8, with no brand.
  • A pre-interview request to log triage time for one week (C7).
  • A request to show two or three recently closed cross-employer items (C11).
  • A randomized price-order card for P5 and P6b.

≈ 8 minSection 1 · Warm-up

Say nothing about software, AI, apps or BuiltNOVA. You are here to learn how their day works.

W1All roles

Walk me through yesterday from the moment you got to the gate until you left. What took most of your attention?

  • What happened right before that?
  • Who did you have to wait on?
Synthetic study suggests

Every respondent's day was dominated by coordination and production pressure; safety arrived as interruptions, not as a task.

Validate with real humans

Whether real crews describe the same shape of day, or whether synthetic agents over-structured it.

W2All rolesKA8 · Validated · 80% V

Who do you have to coordinate with on a normal day, other trades, other contractors or other departments? How does that coordination actually happen?

  • Show me the last message or radio call about it, if you can.
  • Who gets left out of that loop?
Synthetic study suggests

Coordination runs on radio, crew group chats, KakaoTalk/Zalo and the morning foremen's meeting. 'SIMOPS' was Priya's word alone.

Validate with real humans

The actual channels per trade and shift, and who is left out of them (nights, travelers, migrants).

W3All rolesKA3 · Validated · 100% V

When something on site looks off to you or your crew, what typically happens next? Walk me through the most recent example.

  • Who did you tell first?
  • Was anything written down? Where?
Synthetic study suggests

KA3 was the only assumption validated 10/10: hazards are handled by walk-over and word of mouth, then vanish.

Validate with real humans

The ratio of verbal fixes to written reports on a real site; ask to see the last week's written ones.

W4All rolesKA3 · Validated · 100% V

What do you use day to day to keep track of work, permits, observations or issues: paper, radio, phone, software, anything else? What do you like and dislike about each?

  • Which one would you keep if you could keep only one?
Synthetic study suggests

Paper cards and quotas persist only as owner or HQ KPIs. Procore, SafetyCulture and HQ portals are office-side.

Validate with real humans

Which tool is the system of record for each company on site, and who reconciles them.

W5Site managementBuyer / budgetKA5 · Validated · 70% V

How do you find out what happened on site when you weren't there?

  • How many hours later, typically?
  • What do you have to rebuild by hand?
Synthetic study suggests

Managers rely on Monday reconciliation, dashboards and the super's word, and said so openly.

Validate with real humans

How late information arrives, in hours, from a real log.

≈ 32 minSection 2 · Core assessment

Riskiest assumptions first. Ask for the last specific time, not general opinions. Accept silence. Never agree or praise.

C1All rolesKA1 · Validated · 60% VReworded

Tell me about the last time you saw something unsafe that another company created. What did you do, and why that instead of writing it up?

  • What would have happened to you if you had written it up?
  • Who else knew about it?
Synthetic study suggests

All 10 gave a concrete unreported hazard. Main drivers: 'not our hazard', 'told somebody so I'm done', and social or commercial cost between companies. Fear is only part of it.

Validate with real humans

Frequency and base rate of unreported hazards; site managers and buyers answered secondhand (Carlos, Marcus, Bob said so).

C2All rolesKA1 · Validated · 60% VReworded

When someone raises a hazard that involves another company's crew or their own supervisor, what happens to the person who raised it? Can you share an example, good or bad?

  • What did the rest of the crew conclude afterward?
  • What did you do differently after that?
  • [Stewards] How does the union get involved?
Synthetic study suggests

Every retaliation example was quiet and unprovable: reassignment, 'lack of work', non-renewal. Travelers and E-9 workers are most exposed.

Validate with real humans

Real prevalence by segment; whether reporters would believe a technical anonymity guarantee.

C3All rolesKA2 · Partially Validated · 20% VReworded

Think of a hazard you or your crew reported in the last month. What did you see or hear back afterward, and how long did it take?

  • Did the way that one went change whether you reported the next thing?
  • What's the longest you've waited?
Synthetic study suggests

Reporters almost never hear back and learn about fixes by walking past. Nguyen doesn't want to be told, only for it to be fixed.

Validate with real humans

Whether feedback actually raises repeat reporting. Carlos and Marcus called it an untested belief.

C4All rolesKA2 · Partially Validated · 20% VReworded

What has your site or company tried in the past to get more people to report: cards, quotas, rewards, or other programs? What happened to it after the first few weeks?

  • Roughly how many reports in week 1, week 4, week 12?
  • Who stopped first? What did people call it in the break room?
Synthetic study suggests

Every card, raffle and app program decayed within 1-3 months; quotas persist only as KPIs. Nicknames ('snitch cam') predicted death.

Validate with real humans

Real decay curves (Marcus cited 15% of launch week by week 10); which program elements, if any, held.

C5All rolesKA4 · Partially Validated · 44% VReworded

Describe the moment you notice a hazard mid-task: where are you, what's in your hands, what's around you, and how much time do you realistically have to tell someone?

  • If you reported from exactly where you stood, what would stop you first: phone rules, gloves, noise, language, or who might find out?
  • When do you actually get to your phone?
Synthetic study suggests

NA crews: 10-30 s. Phones banned at many work faces (process areas, blocks, IS zones, white space). Tommy and the Korea respondents: consequence or language, not seconds.

Validate with real humans

Observed time-and-motion at the work face; whether reports get made at break with accurate location recall.

C6All rolesKA5 · Validated · 70% V

When a report does get made, walk me through its journey from the person who saw it to the person who fixes it. Where does it slow down, get lost or go to the wrong place?

  • How often is the owner guessed wrong?
  • What happens to it at shift change?
Synthetic study suggests

Location vagueness, ownership guessing (about 1 in 5 wrong, per Carlos), duplicates and night handover are consistent break points.

Validate with real humans

A quantified mis-route and duplicate rate from real Procore or portal exports.

C7Site managementReviewersKA6 · Partially Validated · 0% VReworded

Before we met, I asked you to note how long observation triage took each day last week. Walk me through that log. What information do you need to decide, and what do you get wrong most often?

  • What do you rebuild by hand on Mondays?
  • Who covers nights and weekends?
Synthetic study suggests

Triage load is real: Carlos 5-6 person-hours/day plus 3-4 h Monday; Park 4-5 person-hours/day; Marcus 1-2 h/day per site. Only 4 respondents actually triage.

Validate with real humans

Timed baseline logs; the edit rate reviewers would apply to AI suggestions; night coverage.

N4Site managementReviewersKA6 · Partially Validated · 0% VNew in v2

  • Has volume ever made you stop reading them carefully?
  • Who reviews the reviewer?
Synthetic study suggests

New in v2. The reviewer bottleneck was raised by 9 of 10. KA6 scored 0 of 4 Validated.

Validate with real humans

Actual reviewer capacity, especially nights and weekends, from a timed log.

C8All rolesKA7 · Partially Validated · 0% VReworded

[Show the printed mock item: category, owner, severity, 'confirmed by: J. Smith'.] This item arrived for your area. What do you do with it?

  • What would make you trust or ignore an item like this?
  • After how many wrong ones would you stop looking?
  • [Stewards] Who should be able to see who sent it?
Synthetic study suggests

Trust is lost after 1-3 errors; a named human and a visible reason matter; volume kills trust faster than errors.

C9All rolesKA8 · Validated · 80% VReworded

How many times last week did another crew's work stop yours, or yours stop theirs? Take me through the most recent one.

  • How was it discovered, and by whom?
  • What did it cost in hours?
  • Who owned sorting it out?
Synthetic study suggests

SIMOPS conflicts are frequent (daily to several a week) and cost hours to a day, but respondents rarely said they hit the critical path. The word 'SIMOPS' is Priya's alone.

Validate with real humans

Frequency and cost from permit and lift logs; share that reaches critical path.

C10All rolesKA9 · Partially Validated · 50% VReworded

Tell me about the most recent stop-work, permit revocation or stand-down you were affected by. What led up to it, and what did it cost the schedule?

  • Was the hazard known beforehand? By whom?
  • Who could put a dollar figure on that, and where would they look it up?
Synthetic study suggests

Every stoppage cited had prior verbal knowledge. Costs are big but untracked: no stoppage-hour line, and LDs never attributed.

Validate with real humans

Whether any organization can produce a stoppage-hour baseline; whether buyers would fund measuring it.

C11All rolesKA10 · Validated · 90% V

When a hazard sits on the line between two employers, how does it get closed, and who proves it's closed?

  • Can we look at two or three closed items together? What shows they were fixed?
  • What would an owner or lawyer ask to see?
Synthetic study suggests

'Closed' does not mean fixed: recycled photos, status clicks, block moved. Lawyers ask the same four questions everywhere.

Validate with real humans

A records audit: sample 20 closed cross-employer items and verify in the field.

Authority limits range from $15k/quarter to $500k. IT and security review takes 7 weeks to 5 months. Per-seat is rejected everywhere. Budget cycles close in November.

Actual procurement documents, security questionnaires and contract language (owner-specified tools).

≈ 12 minSection 3 · Concept and willingness to pay

Only now describe the concept. Read the statement verbatim. State the package before any price. Randomize which price the buyer hears first.

Concept statement · read verbatim

Some teams are testing a tool where any worker on site can report a hazard by voice, photo or text in their own language, with no app to install and no login, in about the time it takes to send a text. The system suggests a category, severity and responsible owner. A named human safety reviewer confirms or edits it before it goes anywhere. The owner sees one queue of open items across all employers, and an item only closes when the fix is verified with a time- and location-stamped check. The reporter gets a receipt when it's closed, and their identity is hidden from employers by default. It's priced per project, with unlimited reporting for every worker.

P1All roles

In your own words, what would this change about your week, if anything? What wouldn't it change?

Synthetic study suggests

Most valued: one queue (site management), reporter notified on verified close (Carlos, Tommy, Jamal, Elena), native-language voice (Park, Nguyen). Frontline won't replace walk-overs.

Validate with real humans

Reactions to a working prototype on a real phone, with gloves on, at the work face.

P2All rolesReworded

What worries you about it? Which one of those worries, on its own, would make you or your crew refuse to use it?

Synthetic study suggests

Top fears: identity exposure, reviewer capacity, AI and translation errors, discoverability, device access. They are consistent across roles.

Validate with real humans

Rank-ordering with real users; which fears are deal-breakers versus noise.

P3All roles

Who on your site would use it most, and who would ignore or resist it? Can you introduce me to one of the resisters?

Synthetic study suggests

Likely resisters: area supers, sub owners and presidents, stewards (until bargained), corporate IT, large GCs with enterprise platforms.

Validate with real humans

Talk to the resisters directly (Dale, Tommy, Bob and Priya each named them).

P4Frontline

If this showed up on your site next month, what would decide whether you still used it in month three?

Synthetic study suggests

Month-3 use depends on: it gets fixed, nobody gets burned, and it stays as fast as a text. The 'nobody burned' condition dominates.

Validate with real humans

Only a longitudinal pilot can test this.

P5Site managementBuyer / budgetKA11 · Validated · 100% VReworded

[State the package first] “Unlimited reporting for every worker on site. Licensed users are the reviewers and owners, about 200 people. Integration with your system of record is [included / not included].” Now imagine two ways to pay: a project subscription at [FIRST PRICE] per project-year, or per-seat licensing at about $10 per user per month. How would each land with you, and why?

  • [Reveal second price] Another version is priced at [SECOND PRICE]. How does that compare?
  • Price pair: $150,000 full site and $60,000 single area. Randomize the order, and record it.
Synthetic study suggests

Per-seat rejected 6/6. The '$24k ~200 residents, all employers included' wording confused every buyer: who are the 200?

Validate with real humans

Pricing reaction with an unambiguous scope definition.

P6Site managementBuyer / budgetKA12 · Partially Validated · 0% VReworded

For the package I just described, at what annual price would this be so cheap you'd doubt it? A bargain? Getting expensive but still worth considering? Too expensive no matter what?

  • Would that change if integration were or weren't included?
Synthetic study suggests

WTP points range $25k-$200k (median $95k); ceilings are anchored to headcount cost; answers hinge on whether integration is included.

Validate with real humans

Van Westendorp with n>=30 real buyers per segment, plus Gabor-Granger on a defined package.

P6bSite managementBuyer / budgetKA12 · Partially Validated · 0% VNew in v2

On a scale of 1 to 5, how likely would you be to fund a 12-week pilot at $35,000? At $60,000? What about a full site-year at $95,000, $120,000 or $150,000? What drives the difference?

  • Randomize the order of the price points and record it.
Synthetic study suggests

New in v2. Replaces open-ended price probing with defined price points. Synthetic median WTP point was $95k; bargain median $37.5k.

Validate with real humans

Purchase likelihood by price with n ≥ 30 buyers per segment; the synthetic sample of 6 cannot set a price.

P7Site managementBuyer / budgetKA12 · Partially Validated · 0% V

  • Who else has to say yes: legal, IT security, union, procurement, the owner?
Synthetic study suggests

Evidence lists converge: week 9-12 retention, time to verified close, reviewer edit rate, live integration, security passed, one real 'would-have-been' catch.

Whether real buyers sign on those metrics; who writes the contract clause.

Synthetic study suggests

Frontline would rather have people or physical fixes. Value is conditional on safety and closure. Dollar figures confuse frontline (Nguyen: '$24k is my yearly pay').

Validate with real humans
