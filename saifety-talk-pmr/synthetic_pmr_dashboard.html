Single biggest unmet operational need

Independently verified, cross-employer closure that gets back to the finder. When one company's crew spots a hazard another company created, nobody today assigns a named owner fast, verifies the fix in the field (instead of accepting a status click or a recycled photo), and tells the person who saw it.

10/10respondents described it, across every role and both markets

HeadlinesExecutive Summary

  • The problem is real and specific. The price is not. Hazard signals staying verbal (KA3) was validated 10/10, unowned cross-employer closure (KA10) 9/10 and mis-routing/lag (KA5) 7/10. But 0 of 6 buyer-grade respondents accept $150,000 per project-year as-is (4 conditional, 2 reject). The median 'getting expensive' point is $95,000, and only Bob's WTP point is at or above $150k.
  • The deck's $24,000 price does not rescue it. Only 2 of 6 buyers would accept it. 8 of 10 respondents read '~200 residents' as leaving out the craft who actually see hazards, and 3 buyers (Marcus, Priya, Bob) said the low price made them doubt the vendor would survive.
  • Consequences matter more than seconds. KA4 (the 30-second threshold) is only 44% validated, and the union steward rejected it outright. For Korean migrant crews the barrier is language plus phone bans inside blocks. Identity exposure was raised by 8 of 10. Speed is necessary but not sufficient.
  • The AI-triage core is the least-validated claim. KA6 (1-click review) scored 0 of 4 Validated and KA7 (trust in labeled, human-reviewed routing) 0 of 10 (8 conditional, 2 reject). 9 of 10 raised the human-review bottleneck (who reviews, at night, without rubber-stamping) or volume overload. 'A person always reviews' is table stakes, not a differentiator.
  • Records cut both ways. 7 of 10 raised discoverability: Carlos's deposition fear, Park's SAPA 'exhibit A', Bob's controlling-employer exposure. A tool that records hazards faster than they are closed creates evidence against the buyer. Actions and interim measures have to sit next to every finding.
  • Buyers think in stoppages but won't pay on them. KA9 is only 50% validated. None of the respondents' organizations track stoppage hours or tie LDs to hazards, and Marcus and Bob said 12 weeks can't show it. Measurable value today is triage and reconciliation hours plus time to verified close.
  • The real competitor is a person. WTP ceilings cluster at the loaded cost of a safety coordinator or field rep ($160k-$200k), and 7 of 10 said the money would be better spent on people or physical controls.
  • Split-anchor result (directional only, n=3 per arm): the low-first arm reported higher WTP points (median $120k vs $58k), the opposite of a classic anchoring effect. This is confounded by budget authority (Bob and Priya's owner-side budgets are in arm B; Dale, the schedule-first skeptic, and the Korean division lead are in arm A).

MethodStudy Design Summary

  • Sample: N = 10 synthetic respondents: 4 frontline, 3 site management, 3 buyers. 8 North America, 2 Korea (pilot market). 3 union members.
  • Simulated interview date: 2026-09-27 for all ten. Durations 44-55 minutes. Park and Nguyen were interviewed through interpreters (Korean and Vietnamese).
  • Instrument: Step 20 Key Assumptions and unbiased interview script: warm-up W1-W5, core assessment C1-C12 (ranked riskiest first), Korea module K1-K2 (Park, Nguyen), then the concept and willingness-to-pay section P1-P9. The concept was not mentioned until Section 3. A neutral concept statement was read verbatim. P6 is a Van Westendorp price ladder.
  • Split-anchor design (P5/P5b): Group A (high-first: $150,000 then $24,000) = Carlos, Dale, Park, plus frontline Elena and Jamal. Group B (low-first: $24,000 then $150,000) = Priya, Marcus, Bob, plus frontline Tommy and Nguyen. Frontline heard both figures inside a single P8 question, so the anchor split effectively does not apply to them.
  • Pricing discrepancy to resolve: Deck slide 9 prices at $24,000 per project-year (200 residents x $10/user/month) and describes $150,000 as a two-year discretionary HSE budget on a $500M EPC project. The founder brief tests a $150,000/year base (range $75k-$225k). Both anchors were tested. The deck also says 'per project, not per seat' while deriving its price from a per-seat formula, and respondents noticed (see Surprises).

Scoring rubric

  • V (Validated): the respondent volunteered the behavior or pain before the concept, with a specific recent example. For concept-stage assumptions (KA7, KA11, KA12), V means clear, unconditional support.
  • P (Partially validated): the pain exists but is weaker, conditional, secondhand without a specific example, or only confirmed after probing.
  • I (Invalidated): the respondent contradicts the assumption, or it doesn't apply to their reality.
  • N/A (shown as – in the matrix): not asked, or not applicable to the role. KA6 applies only to people who actually triage observations. KA11 and KA12 apply only to buyer-grade respondents. Bob declined to estimate crew reporting time (KA4). N/A is excluded from the denominator.
  • Verdict rule (applied mechanically): Validated if V ≥ 60% of applicable n. Invalidated if I ≥ 50%, or if V+P < 40%. Otherwise Partially Validated.
  • Judgment calls, stated openly: KA9 is scored on the whole claim. Frontline respondents get V if they gave a known hazard → stoppage → schedule example. Buyers get V only if they also frame value in stoppage or float terms. KA8 gets V for frequent SIMOPS stoppages with a time cost; Dale gets P because he explicitly said they usually don't hit the critical path. KA2 gets V only where there is positive evidence that visible closure sustains reporting, not just that gamification fails.
  • WTP: scored only for the six buyer-grade respondents. Frontline answers are recorded as perceived value.

B4. Surprises: Things the Founder Did Not Assume

  • A low price reduces credibility. 3 of 6 buyers (Marcus, Priya, Bob) said $24k made them suspect the vendor won't survive, integration isn't really included, or it won't pass security. For Bob, $24k is below his 'too cheap' line.
  • '~200 residents' reads as 'the craft aren't covered'. 8 of 10 raised it without being prompted. Carlos and Park independently proposed the fix: license reviewers and owners, and let everyone report for free.
  • The deck's pricing logic contradicts its own message. Slide 9 says 'per project, not per seat', then derives $24k as 200 seats × $10. Buyers immediately asked 'who are the 200?'
  • Seconds are not the binding constraint. Tommy: 'The consequence is the problem.' Nguyen and Park: language, plus no phones inside blocks. Jamal, Priya, Marcus, Bob, Elena: phones or photos banned in process areas, IS zones and white space. Reporting will mostly happen at break, so location recall matters more than speed.
  • Notifying the reporter can be dangerous. Nguyen: a 'your report is closed' alert on his phone next to his 반장 would expose him. He also said 'I don't need them to tell me', only that it gets fixed. KA2's mechanism (notification drives retention) may not hold for the most vulnerable workers.
  • Anonymity is technically hard. In a crew of 12 with only 8 Vietnamese workers, 'it's easy to guess who' (Nguyen). A photo of a location only one crew works in identifies the reporter (Jamal). Tuan or the 반장 can recognize a voice (Nguyen). Supervisors, on the other hand, need to identify reporters to follow up (Carlos, Park).
  • Owner-side legal risk. Holding 'one queue of open items' may push an owner toward OSHA controlling-employer status (Bob). In Korea, routing tasks from the prime to subcontractor workers risks illegal dispatch (불법파견) (Park). Both constrain the core 'owner sees one queue' promise.
  • The reverse closure failure. Dale's complaint is items that were fixed but sat open in Procore for three weeks, making his supers look bad in the owner report. Stale 'open' is as politically toxic as false 'closed'.
  • The competitor is a headcount, not software. Carlos ($160k ceiling = a loaded coordinator), Marcus ($175k), Bob ($200k = a field rep), Elena (two walking safety staff), Tommy (a lift coordinator), Nguyen (fans and a Vietnamese safety person), Jamal (more crew).
  • 'What's the rule here?' splits along power lines. It's the feature the most vulnerable frontline respondents would use most, because asking isn't reporting (Jamal, Nguyen). It's the feature managers fear most (Priya: a hallucinated permit; Carlos, Marcus, Park, Tommy: the wrong rule hierarchy, e.g., OSHA vs corporate vs owner vs CBA vs KOSHA).
  • The pre-task or TBM is the natural integration point. Elena and Nguyen, unprompted, said the morning briefing is where open hazards should show up. Nguyen understands only half of the TBM, which may matter more than reporting.
  • The contract is the real gate. Bob: 'that's the real gate: the contract.' Marcus: owner contract requirements are how most tools get adopted, and a week versus five months of budget conversation. Mid-job adoption is a favor.
  • Stoppage hours aren't measured anywhere. No PMIS or cost code for safety-caused delay (Bob, Marcus, Dale: 'Nobody writes safety next to it'). In Korea the cost 'is forgotten when the next year's budget is made' (Park). And for Nguyen, 'the stop didn't change the deadline, it only changed our sleep': the delay was absorbed as overtime and fatigue.
  • The existing card or near-miss quota won't go away. It's an owner-report or HQ KPI (Carlos, Park, Dale). Unless reports count toward it, the tool stacks, and stacking was named as fatal.
  • 'SIMOPS' is buyer vocabulary. Only Priya used the word. Crews describe the same thing as trades 'stacked', a load 'swinging over my guys', or 'the grinding team was already inside'.

North America vs Korea

  • Barrier to reporting: NA = consequence plus friction (logins, gloves, noise). KR = language first, then fear of the 반장 and the subcontractor president, then the physical impossibility of reporting from inside a block.
  • Retaliation mechanism: NA = reassignment, 'lack of work', 'end of assignment'. KR = contract or E-9 non-renewal, penalty points to the subcontractor, 'Next time, just put a rope yourself and don't make noise'.
  • Legal frame: NA = OSHA citation or deposition, controlling employer. KR = SAPA criminal liability for executives, MoEL work-stop orders, KakaoTalk forensics, PIPA, illegal dispatch.
  • Buying: NA authority ranges $15k/quarter to $500k; GMP-reimbursable. KR team lead is capped at about $36k (KRW 50M); vendor registration takes about 2 months; ISMS-P and in-country data are non-negotiable; the unit is division-year, not project; the budget plan closes around November.
  • Incumbent channel: NA = radio, crew group text, WhatsApp, Procore. KR = KakaoTalk (managers), Zalo (Vietnamese workers), the HQ smart-safety portal.

Union vs non-union

  • Union (Elena IBEW, Tommy Iron Workers, Jamal UA): there is an institutional veto (steward, business agent, labor-management committee) and a known route to acceptance: bargain it in writing, no discipline, a real anonymity option, union access to aggregate data, no personal-phone installs. Tommy: 'The paper decides it.' Being in a union doesn't remove fear. Jamal (a traveler) still won't go over his foreman, and Elena still weighs being 'the one who complains'.
  • Non-union / at-will / visa (Carlos's open-shop subs, Bob's Texas site, Nguyen's E-9 status): retaliation is silent and unprovable ('this is Texas, non-union and at-will... nobody writes down why it was those forty', Bob). There is no veto holder to negotiate with, so protection has to be built into the product itself.
